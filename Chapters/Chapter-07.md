# Chapter 7

The seven years passed.

Jianxin read everything he could find. Not the assigned texts, though he read those too, but the ones that had no business being in an institute library, fables and folk tales, histories of people who had lived and been forgotten, records of disputes and loves and small catastrophes that had mattered enormously to the people inside them and not at all to anyone else. He read them the way he had learned to read Garson, looking past the words at the mind that had arranged them, and in the arrangement he found the edges of intent he could work with.

He built sword arts out of them. The technique of a foolish fisherman who kept casting his net in the wrong waters until one day he didn't. The guard of a woman who had written letters to someone who never wrote back, all that patience in her brushwork, all that contained wanting. A story about two brothers who couldn't agree on how to divide their father's land, and the movement that emerged from the specific shape of that argument, both of them unable to quite bring themselves to win.

The sword arts were strange. The instructors watched him practice them with expressions Jianxin couldn't read, which happened more and more as the years passed. They were like trying to read water, something moved beneath the surface, something with weight and direction, but the moment he reached for it the surface closed over and he was left with nothing. After the third year he stopped trying. Whatever the instructors were, they were more than they looked, and he filed that away in the category of things to think about later.

What he could do was watch Tianyi with them, and from Tianyi he could read plenty.

Tianyi, by the fourth year, had become something remarkable even by the standards of people who had been told all their lives that he would be remarkable. He moved through domains the way he moved through the early Go games, absorbing the structure of a thing until he could generate from it, until the map drew itself forward. Alchemy arrived in a month. Formation theory in three weeks. A visiting instructor in celestial cartography stayed for six days and left looking slightly unsettled, having apparently expected to do more teaching. A clan elder came to assess his medical cultivation, spent two days with him, and left without sharing her findings with anyone, which Jianxin noted and filed. The cooking instructor, the one who had been brought specifically to teach medicinal cuisine and who had initially seemed the most unimpressed by either of them, stopped giving Tianyi corrections after the third session and started asking him questions instead.

The domains kept coming. Each one arrived, was absorbed, and within weeks he was producing things the original practitioners hadn't reached. Not someone who had learned a thing. Someone who had understood it deeply enough to find its edges and push past them.

Jianxin watched this for seven years and still found it funny. Not in a diminishing way. Something genuinely absurd didn't stop being absurd just because you'd seen it before.

Jianxin watched his brother and felt something settle in him, not resignation exactly, more like the satisfaction of understanding something that had always been true. The domains he would never touch, the ones that required systematic comprehension, the ones that rewarded accumulation and analysis, those were Tianyi's, had always been Tianyi's, would remain so for as long as they lived. He had made peace with this sometime around the fifth year.

What he had was the sword, and the sword was enough, because the sword had no ceiling he could see.

By sixteen Jianxin had stopped thinking of combat as something that happened between bodies and had started thinking of it as a conversation between intents. The flow of a fight moved the way a conversation moved, currents and eddies, a statement demanding a response, silence that meant something different depending on what came before it. He could feel the shape of an exchange before it resolved, the next move already present before it arrived. Against the instructors in practice he lost consistently and without learning much, their intents too deep and too settled to grip. Against Tianyi he won consistently and with great interest, because Tianyi's mind had become the most complex thing available to him and he had spent seven years learning its texture.

The only irritant, and it was a small one, was that Tianyi's calculations had grown to the point where some of what Jianxin did in the early exchanges was no longer invisible. Not all of it. But enough that the margin of mystery had narrowed, and that was something worth paying attention to.

Tianyi, for his part, had stopped asking what he was missing and started asking how it worked. The distinction was subtle but Jianxin felt it. That was a more interesting problem and a more dangerous one.

The Go boards gathered dust in the common room. By unspoken agreement they hadn't played since the night of Jianxin's first win, both of them unwilling to find out something they weren't ready to know yet.

As the years passed the awareness of what was coming settled into the background of everything, present the way a sound is present when you've stopped hearing it. They were being prepared for something. The institute existed for a purpose and the purpose had a date and the date was getting closer. Sometimes Jianxin would be in the middle of an ordinary thing — sparring, reading, eating — and feel it there underneath, the specific pressure of a life that was ending and another that hadn't started yet. He didn't find it heavy. He found it interesting, the way a held breath is interesting, the moment before the thing happens.

---

The last week was different.

Nothing changed exactly. The schedule was the same, the instructors were the same, the institute's corridors had the same light at the same hours. But Jianxin found himself noticing things he'd stopped noticing years ago. The way the stained glass threw its colors across the arena floor in the early morning, the warriors and sages rendered in fragments of light that shifted as the day moved. He'd walked through that light a thousand times. Now he walked through it slowly.

The common room table had a wobbly left leg that had been wobbly for as long as either of them could remember. Someone had wedged a folded piece of paper under it at some point and the paper had compressed and the wobble had returned and no one had fixed it. Jianxin noticed it for what felt like the first time and then immediately remembered noticing it dozens of times before, and couldn't account for how it had become invisible again between then and now.

Tianyi spent more time in the library. Not studying. Jianxin checked once and found him sitting at a table with a closed book in front of him, looking at nothing. He left without saying anything.

The instructors seemed to know. They didn't say anything either, but their sessions that final week were slightly different, something more present in them, as if they too were paying attention to things they'd stopped noticing. The sword instructor held a correction for a half-second longer than usual. The formations instructor answered a question Tianyi asked with more detail than the question required. Small things. Nothing you could point to.

The night before their birthday Jianxin couldn't sleep.

He lay in the dark for an hour and then gave up and went to the arena. The stained glass was dark at this hour, just colored panes without light behind them, but the space itself was the same, the worn stone floor, the high ceiling, the smell of years of practice built into the walls. He sat down in the center of it and did nothing for a while.

Tianyi appeared in the doorway twenty minutes later.

"I thought you'd be here," he said.

"I thought you'd be asleep."

"I was." He came and sat down a few feet away, not close enough to be a comfort, close enough to be present. "Then I wasn't."

They sat in the dark arena for a while without speaking. This was not uncomfortable. They had spent enough time in this room together that silence in it had its own texture, different from silence elsewhere.

"Are you ready?" Jianxin asked.

Tianyi thought about it properly. "I've been preparing for this for seven years. Theoretically I should be more prepared than I'll ever be." A pause. "Theoretically."

"But."

"But preparation and readiness aren't the same thing." He looked at the dark glass. "I know what I know. I don't know what I don't know. Out there the second category is much larger than it's ever been in here."

Jianxin thought about this. "That's true for me too."

"It's different for you. Your path runs on encounter. The unknown is the material." Tianyi was quiet for a moment. "Mine runs on comprehension. I need to understand things before I can work with them. Out there I'm going to be working with things I don't understand yet." Another pause. "Constantly."

"You'll figure it out."

"Yes," Tianyi said. "That's not what concerns me. What concerns me is the time between not knowing and figuring it out."

Jianxin looked at him. "You'll have me for that part."

Tianyi didn't say anything for a moment. Then: "That helps."

It wasn't much. It was enough.

They sat in the arena until the sky began to lighten, not talking much, just present in the space they'd shared for seven years, and when the first grey light started coming through the glass Jianxin stood up and stretched and said they should probably sleep for at least an hour, and Tianyi said that was statistically unlikely to improve cognitive function at this point, and Jianxin said he knew but they should try anyway, and Tianyi followed him back to their rooms, and they did not sleep, and the knock came before dawn.

---

The morning of their sixteenth birthday the senior instructor came to their rooms.

He was the one who had been there since the beginning, who occasionally stood in the garden at dusk looking at the sloped horizon with an expression Jianxin had never been able to read. He knocked once and they both opened their doors at the same time, which he seemed to expect.

"It's time," he said. "Take what you need. Leave what you don't."

That was all.

---

Jianxin's room took five minutes to pack. His sword went on his back first, then the Garson manual, then the three storybooks he hadn't finished with yet, then clothes. He looked around at what remained, the desk where the candle had burned down to nothing seven years ago, the cot, the shelf of assigned texts he'd read and wouldn't need again, and felt very little about leaving them. They were furniture. The things that mattered were already in his bag.

He heard Tianyi moving in the next room, the methodical sounds of someone who had thought about this in advance. Which Tianyi had, probably. He'd likely made a list.

Jianxin sat on the edge of the cot and waited.

After a few minutes Tianyi appeared in the doorway, his bag over one shoulder, a small sealed package in his other hand that hadn't been there yesterday. The clan's delivery, arrived overnight without anyone delivering it. Tianyi set it on the desk without opening it and looked at Jianxin.

For a moment neither of them said anything. The room was quieter than usual mornings.

"Big day," Jianxin said.

"Yes."

"You ready?"

"We've been through this."

"Humor me."

Tianyi looked at him. "No. Are you?"

Jianxin thought about the arena the night before, the dark glass, the worn floor. "Also no." He stood up. "Let's go anyway."

"You know you don't have to come," Tianyi said.

Jianxin looked at him. "Good morning to you too."

"I mean it."

"Come where? We're leaving the institute. We're both leaving the institute. That's what's happening this morning."

"You know what I mean."

"I really don't." Jianxin tilted his head. "Why don't you explain it to me."

Tianyi looked at him with the patience of someone who has decided not to be played. "You figured it out. Probably years ago. That your path isn't bound by the same restriction mine is, that the outside Dao doesn't threaten you the way it threatens me, that you could go directly to the clan's territory or anywhere else and it wouldn't matter." He said it without inflection. "I've known for a while that you knew. So stop pretending you don't."

Jianxin held the look for another moment, then let it go. "Around year four," he said. "Give or take."

Something in Tianyi's posture shifted, barely perceptible. "And."

"And what?"

"What did you decide."

Jianxin looked at him for a moment. Then he stood up and picked up his bag. "Come on, we're going to be late."

"Jianxin."

"I didn't decide anything." He said it simply, without drama. "There wasn't a decision. You're my brother. Where you go I go, that's just what's true. The clan can have opinions about it."

Tianyi was quiet for a long moment. Outside the window the sky had begun to lighten, the deep blue shifting toward something paler. He picked up the sealed package from the desk.

"You could have gone to see our parents," he said. "Years ago."

"I know."

"That doesn't bother you."

"No," Jianxin said. "Not really."

Tianyi looked at him for a moment, as if checking whether that was true. It was.

Tianyi looked at him with the attention he reserved for things he was trying to understand fully. Then he tucked the package into his bag and shouldered it.

"I'll pay you back," he said. "For this."

"You make the gruel when I get hurt. We're even."

"That's not the same."

"It's close enough." Jianxin grinned. "Come on."

They walked out together, down the corridor that had been theirs for seven years, past the common room where the Go boards sat dusty and unplayed on their shelf, through the main hall where the morning light was just beginning to come through the stained glass windows, painting its fractured colors on the stone floor one last time. Jianxin didn't stop to look at it. He'd looked at it enough.

They walked through the gate together.

Behind them, the institute was gone. Not demolished, not abandoned, simply no longer present. The lot stood empty, the stones already absorbed back into the ground, the stained glass warriors and ancient sages dissolved into the morning air. Seven years of walls and classrooms and the common room table with its wobbly left leg, all of it returned to wherever the clan's things went when they were finished being needed.

Jianxin turned to look once, found only empty ground, and turned back.

Tianyi hadn't turned at all.
