# Chapter 4

The common room was small enough that the board took up most of the table. They'd played here so many times that sitting down had a quality of returning to something, the same two chairs, the same slight wobble in the table's left leg that neither of them had ever bothered to fix.

Tianyi set out the stones with the efficiency of someone who had performed a ritual long enough that the ritual itself had become automatic. Jianxin dropped into his chair and propped his chin in his hand, watching.

"You go first," Tianyi said.

"I always go first."

"You always insist on going first."

"Same thing." Jianxin picked up a stone and turned it between his fingers. "Different connotation."

He placed the first stone and settled back, and tried to do what he'd been thinking about since calligraphy.

He reached for Tianyi the way he'd reached for Garson, looking for the mind underneath the moves. With Garson it had taken hours. With the calligrapher a shift of temperature had been enough. He'd expected Tianyi, of all people, to be easier.

With Tianyi he got nothing he didn't already have.

He got everything he already knew, twelve years of his brother, recognition rather than discovery, and recognition wasn't useful here. There was no new angle. Just Tianyi, as familiar as his own reflection, seen from the same direction he'd always seen him.

He tried to push past the familiarity toward the structure underneath it.

The board received Tianyi's first stone. Clean placement, center-weighted, the opening of someone who intended to build outward from a position of control rather than contest the edges. Jianxin had seen this opening hundreds of times. He responded without thinking and kept reaching.

What he found, when he stopped looking for something new, was the thing he already knew in a form he hadn't quite articulated before. Tianyi's mind on the board didn't experience it as a conversation between two players. It experienced the board as a problem with a correct answer at every position, one that could be approached asymptotically through sufficiently deep calculation. Jianxin wasn't an opponent to be read. He was a variable in a system, one whose behavior could be modeled and accounted for.

It was completely Tianyi, and it told him almost nothing useful. A variable in a system didn't have desires to exploit, no private certainties underneath the moves the way there'd been certainty underneath Garson's rulings. Just calculation, going deeper with every exchange.

He placed a stone and watched Tianyi consider it, and felt the approach fail quietly, the way a door fails when you've been leaning on it and it simply isn't there.

Fine. He stopped reaching and just played.

For several moves he let himself settle back into the game as he'd always known it, reading the board rather than his brother, responding to positions rather than intentions. It felt like returning to solid ground. He was good at this. He was very good at this, in the way that didn't come from calculation but from something more like fluency, the board speaking a language he'd been absorbing since childhood.

Tianyi built his web across the middle game with the same patience he brought to everything. No aggression, no overcommitment. Lines of pressure extending across multiple regions simultaneously, each one individually answerable but collectively beginning to weigh. Jianxin had grown up inside that web. He knew its texture the way you know the particular quality of a recurring dream.

He placed a stone in the upper left corner that looked like territory consolidation.

It wasn't.

He hadn't planned it exactly. It had come from knowing his brother, from twelve years of drowning in that particular web. Tianyi liked his pressure distributed, never committing to a single decisive threat when he could maintain five inconclusive ones simultaneously. To do that effectively his attacking lines needed to converge, and there was a point in the upper left where two of his natural convergences would eventually want to meet.

The stone sat there and said nothing.

Tianyi looked at it briefly, then placed his own stone elsewhere. The game continued.

It was in the middle of the next sequence that Jianxin noticed something.

He'd been watching Tianyi's moves with the ordinary attention he brought to games, tracking the shape of the board, and he caught it almost by accident. Tianyi was still building his web with the same patience, but something in the pattern had changed slightly. The lines were angling away from the upper left. Not dramatically, not in a way that would have been visible in isolation. Just a consistent, small deflection, the kind that accumulates across moves until you realize the web you thought was everywhere has a region it keeps not quite entering.

Tianyi had seen the stone. Not as a threat yet, not consciously enough to respond to directly. But somewhere in the calculation it had registered as a future problem, and the web was bending around it the way water bends around a stone in a riverbed.

Jianxin placed another stone and felt Tianyi's attention shift slightly.

There it was. Diffuse intention beginning to cohere around a point.

He reached again, not for the familiar shape of his brother's mind but for this specific thing, this narrowing, and found it much easier to hold. Intent needed something to organize around. A diffuse mind was unreadable. A mind beginning to focus had direction, and direction could be read.

He wasn't sure yet what to do with that. But it was the most interesting thing he'd found all week, which for Jianxin was as good as a reason.

He watched Tianyi calculate. The stone wasn't an immediate threat, didn't block anything directly. But it sat where two of his attacking convergences would eventually need to meet, and somewhere in the deeper calculation that had registered. To execute his preferred strategy, he would need to deal with it. Dealing with it would cost moves he'd rather spend elsewhere. Not dealing with it would cost him the convergences he needed to close out the game.

The balancing act had begun without Tianyi having decided to begin it.

Over the following moves Jianxin watched his brother try to hold both pressures at once and felt the quality of the game change. Tianyi was still playing well, better than almost anyone Jianxin had ever faced. But he was no longer playing the accumulation game he preferred, the one where he never had to commit because the pressure was always everywhere. He was playing around a fixed point now, and fixed points were legible in a way distributed pressure never was.

Jianxin could see where the intent was going, not because he could calculate that far but because he could feel which way it was leaning, the way you feel a conversation turning before the words that turn it have been said.

He placed his stones to keep the balancing act alive, making sure neither commitment was ever quite enough. Every move that strengthened the web weakened the response to the stone. He wasn't playing the board anymore. He was playing the mind playing the board, and the mind was readable because he'd given it something to read.

The game closed out over the next twenty moves. Tianyi never made an obvious error. He just ran out of good responses, the web slowly smothered by the cost of the balancing act, the stone in the corner becoming a drain that had been pulling resources from everything else since the middle game.

Tianyi looked at the board for a long moment after the final stone.

"When did you place that," he said. Not a question exactly.

"Upper left? Twelve moves before you noticed it."

Tianyi was quiet, tracing the game backward in his head. Jianxin could see him doing it, the slight stillness that meant full processing.

"I want to learn Go properly," Tianyi said finally.

Jianxin looked at him. "You know Go properly."

"I want to learn it without you in it." He stood and began collecting stones with the same efficiency he'd set them out with. "There's something in the way you play that I can't account for yet. I'd rather understand the game first and then account for you separately."

He said it the way he said most things, without heat or frustration, as a straightforward observation about an optimization problem. But Jianxin knew him well enough to know that wanting to remove a variable from an equation was as close as Tianyi got to admitting the variable was beating him.

"It'll still be there when you come back," Jianxin said. "The thing you can't account for."

"Maybe." Tianyi set the last stone in its bowl. "Or maybe I'll have closed the gap enough that it doesn't matter."

He left without waiting for a response, which was also very Tianyi.

Jianxin sat with the empty board for a moment. He wasn't thinking about the game. He was thinking about what it meant that reading Tianyi had required making Tianyi readable first, that understanding his brother had needed him to change the conditions rather than simply look harder. He didn't know yet whether that said something about Tianyi specifically or about people in general. He suspected it was both, and that figuring out which was going to take longer than one evening.
