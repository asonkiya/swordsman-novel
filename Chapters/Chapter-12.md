# Chapter 12

The courtyard was empty when Jianxin came down at dawn. He sat against the eastern wall with the sword across his knees and didn't draw it yet.

He'd been thinking about the spirit root again.

It sat in him untouched and patient, a heaven grade root the clan had confirmed before he could walk. The highest orthodox grade there was. People built entire lineages around roots two tiers below it. He'd been told what it meant before he was old enough to weigh it, a fact about himself he'd had no part in choosing, and the choice of what to do with it had been waiting for him at the gate when he walked out of the institute.

The clan had made their position clear the morning they left. Not through the senior instructor, who had said almost nothing, but through the sealed package Tianyi had found on his desk, which had contained among other things a letter addressed to Jianxin that laid out the situation plainly, without warmth or pressure, facts for someone old enough to be responsible for them.

There had also been a cultivation manual. A sword daoist path, tailored for a heaven grade root, orthodox and proven and mapped all the way to the upper realms. He'd read it in two hours, understood it completely, and set it aside. It was a good manual. The problem was that it made him a Daoist who used the sword, and that was the wrong order. The sword was the thing. Whatever else came would come through the sword, or it wouldn't come at all.

The clan found this difficult to understand. Not angry, exactly. The clan didn't do anger, or didn't do it where he could see. But the instructors had made the position clear over the years, in the spaces between other things.

Cultivation built on what you brought to it. An edge secured at the very bottom, a single percent of advantage at the start, did not stay a single percent. It compounded through the realms until it became a difference in kind, two cultivators who began a hair apart standing in entirely different leagues by the time they reached the heights. This was why the grade of a root mattered so much. It was not the size of the head start. Cultivators had never lacked for time. It was that the advantage itself grew, magnified by every realm it passed through, until what began as a small thing became the whole distance between one fate and another.

And the orthodox path had been built with the Dao in mind. Shaped over uncounted generations to run in accordance with the way reality actually worked, every realm a step that the Dao itself seemed to have laid down in advance. To walk it was to walk with the grain of the world. Tianyi, with his physique, was very nearly the path made into a person, his comprehension and the orthodox route the same thing pointed in the same direction.

A heaven grade root set on that path would have gone far. The clan knew it. They had told him what he was declining, not as a threat but as a fact he should carry knowingly: that he had stepped off the road built in accordance with the Dao and onto something with no map, no precedent, and no guarantee, and that the deviation was his and would remain his.

He understood the argument. He'd simply never found it persuasive enough to move him.

Tianyi had started cultivating in the forest, and Tianyi was already pulling ahead. Jianxin could feel it without trying, a density to his brother that hadn't been there a week ago, the orthodox path doing exactly what the clan said it did. For the first time since they were nine, the gap between them was widening instead of closing, and it would keep widening as long as Jianxin sat in courtyards with a sword he'd built out of storybooks while his brother climbed a ladder that had no top.

He thought about this honestly. It was motivating, in a way. It made him want to get better.

It didn't make him want to use the root.

He stood, drew the sword, and got to work.

---

He started with the front-pusher's sword.

He'd watched it on the road and now he swung it, or tried to, the same overhand cuts the bandit had thrown at the escort's guard, heavy and committed and a half-beat too eager. He ran the motion until his arm knew it, until the blade fell the same as the man's blade had fallen.

It rang hollow.

Not the form. The form was correct. The motion had the right weight, the right angle, the shoulder going into it as the bandit's had. But it felt as though the sword was asking him: why.

Why this swing. Why this weight.

He thought about the shoulder. The whole shoulder going into a stroke that only needed the forearm. He'd seen the Vayne escorts move, efficient, trained, every motion sized to its purpose. This hadn't been that. This was the swing of a man who had spent years driving something heavier than a sword, who had built his arms for a different tool and carried that building into a fight it was never shaped for. An axe maybe, or a hoe, or the haft of a cart.

Why was a laboring man on that road.

He swung again and let the question sit. Tianyi had said someone sent them. Thirty-two men didn't appear on a road by accident. They'd been paid. So this man had been given money to be there.

What had he done with it.

The blade was the first answer. He'd seen the checkpoint guards' steel and the convoy guards' steel and the Vayne escorts' steel. He had a gradient now. This man's blade had been nicked along the edge, the grip rewrapped badly over old cord, a sword bought from someone who needed to sell it rather than chosen for the hand that would use it. A man who had received an advance for a dangerous job and had not spent it on the tool that might keep him alive.

Which meant he'd spent it on something he wanted more immediately.

What had he wanted.

He thought about the fight. The man's eyes had been red at the edges, not from the exertion. They'd been red before anything started, carrying a brightness of someone running on something. His movements had had an agitation underneath them, a tremor in the commitment, the kind that came from a body being pushed past its natural state. Not fear. Something taken.

So: money spent, and spent recently, on something that cost enough to matter and burned fast enough to need replacing. Not food. The people in the lower districts who had been going without had shown it plainly, hollow cheeks, clothes hanging off shoulders that had been wider once, the kind of careful movement that came from a body rationing itself. This man had none of that. His shoulders had filled his tunic. His neck had been thick. His arms had the kind of bulk that came from years of consistent labor and consistent eating, not from scraping by. He'd been feeding himself fine. The money hadn't gone to survival.

It had gone to pleasure. The kind that ran out and had to be bought again.

And in the fight his whole body had pointed past the escorts at the carriage. Not at the men between him and it. At the thing inside. At money. The thing he kept spending faster than he could earn it, the thing he was on this road to get more of, the thing that had apparently been the organizing principle of his life for long enough that even his sword swings were reaching past their target toward it.

He hadn't been fighting. He'd been reaching. The sword was only the nearest tool.

Jianxin swung and the sword was different.

A servant crossing the courtyard with an armload of linen glanced over at the boy practicing forms, and then slowed without meaning to. The boy was only swinging a sword. But there was something in it. The first strokes had been a young man going through motions, clean and empty. These were not empty. Each cut reached past where it landed, leaning toward something past the end of the blade, hungry with something a sword had no business carrying, and the servant could not have said what was different but felt the difference and stood holding the linen a moment too long before moving on.

Jianxin didn't notice her. He was somewhere else, the courtyard thinned to almost nothing around him, a carriage at the end of every stroke that wasn't there, the want of a dead man bleeding faintly into the motion of his arm. Not vivid. A flavor, a lean, the ghost of another person's reaching. But it was his now, the thing he'd watched on the road folded down into the blade, ready to come back out when he called it.

He held it a while, swinging, letting it thicken. Then he set it down and reached for the next one.

---

The afraid one was harder.

The reconstruction came easily enough. A younger man, soft hands, clothes that had never been good, the look of someone who'd fallen in with the wrong company recently rather than fighting his way down over years. New to this. He'd held his sword like someone who'd been shown how to hold it last month. And during the fight his eyes had kept going to the treeline and his weight had kept shifting back, the whole of him already leaving while the trained surface of him kept going through the motions.

But when Jianxin swung for him the notion that came was split. Two directions at once. The forward motion of a man still committed to the fight and the backward pull of a man already gone, and they fought each other inside the single stroke, and the blade wanted to do two incompatible things and could do neither cleanly. He swung a dozen times and never got it to settle. The man himself had never settled. That was the whole of him, a person divided against his own action, and the sword that came from him carried the division and the division wouldn't resolve because it hadn't resolved in life.

Jianxin found this more interesting than the front-pusher, in the end. The clean ones told you who a person was. The divided ones told you where a person was breaking.

He set it down too, unfinished. Some things were truer unfinished.

---

He moved through the others as the morning warmed.

The one who'd watched the man on the horse, looking for permission before every move, a follower so far down into following that his intent had no center of its own, only an orientation toward whoever held authority. The sword from him pointed always at someone offstage. The big quiet one near the back who hadn't wanted to be there and hadn't known how to leave, whose every motion had been an apology. The two who'd moved well together, brothers maybe, or close enough, their footwork accounting for each other without either one looking. He couldn't get a clean read on either alone. They only made sense as a pair, and he filed that as a problem to come back to.

Some of them gave him almost nothing. He'd seen them for half a second in the chaos and the reconstruction was more guess than reading, and the notions that came from those swings were thin and probably wrong, built from assumption rather than observation. He kept those separate in his mind, marked them as uncertain. A sword built on a guess would fail when it met the truth.

Fourteen of them were dead.

He came to that quietly, somewhere in the middle of the morning, working through a man he'd put down himself. The reconstruction worked the same as the others, the clothes and the hands and the history, the notion gathering at the edge of the blade. But this one would never do anything to contradict it. The living men he was reading might still surprise him, might turn out to be something other than what he'd deduced. This one couldn't. The snapshot was the whole of him now, fixed, the ceiling and the floor in the same place.

He held the swing a moment longer than the others. Then he moved on.

---

By midmorning the household had woken around him.

Staff crossed the courtyard on errands. A groom led horses past the gate. A pair of kitchen girls carried something heavy between them and argued cheerfully about whose fault its weight was. Jianxin kept working, and as he worked he reached for them too, idly, without deciding to.

They were different. The thirty-two were fixed points, each one a single intent caught and held. The living people moving through the courtyard were nothing like fixed. Their intents shifted moment to moment, the groom's attention sliding from the horses to a girl across the yard to the ache in his own back, the kitchen girls' argument carrying six small intents a second under the words. He couldn't catch them and hold them as he caught the dead men. They wouldn't sit still long enough. He could only read them as they moved, the reading dissolving and reforming with each shift.

He liked it better. The dead men were a study. The living ones were the thing itself.

He was deep in a form, half his attention on a swing built from the front-pusher and half on a stablehand carrying tack across the far side of the yard, when the world changed.

It didn't come as a sound or a sight. It came as a pressure under everything, a primal sense older than intent-reading, the kind of thing every living person carried whether they cultivated or not, the animal awareness of where the weight in a room was. Jianxin had always felt it faintly. Now it pulled at him like a tide.

Something changed in his bones.

Not a sound. Not a sight. A pressure that had nothing to do with the air, felt somewhere below the skin, the specific sensation of the world tilting slightly toward one point. He'd felt something like it before, in childhood sparring, when the air around Tianyi would thicken and every strike would meet invisible resistance, when the ground seemed to answer his brother's stance and the wind seemed to know which way he wanted it to go. That had been a tilt. This was not a tilt.

The qi in the courtyard was moving. Not violently, not visibly, but he could feel it in his sternum, in his back teeth, in the hand wrapped around the sword, every mote of it organizing itself toward the courtyard door, not pushed, just finding the direction it was always going to find. The grass near the door lay flat. The groom had stopped walking, his horse's ears back. The kitchen girls had gone quiet without knowing why.

Jianxin lowered his sword and turned.

Tianyi stood at the courtyard door, and the world around him was not the same world that filled the rest of the yard. The air answered him. The qi arranged itself in his presence as it always had, but denser now, more total, the fire in the nearby brazier bending faintly toward him without wind to explain it, the ground under his feet more present than the ground under anyone else's. He looked faintly tired and entirely himself.

"Fourth level," he said. "It went faster here. The qi is denser inside the walls."

Jianxin looked at his brother and felt the weight of him in his own teeth and sternum, and understood that this was not simply Tianyi growing stronger.

He had stepped into a different order of being. The world had already reorganized itself to acknowledge it, and Jianxin's bones had known before he turned around.

For the first time in years, he knew with complete certainty that if they fought right now, he would lose. Not narrowly. Completely.
