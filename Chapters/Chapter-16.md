# Chapter 16

The comment hung in the air, and the room waited to see what would be done with it.

Jianxin felt the attention land on him. He was the one who had been insulted, or the nearest thing to it, the help who'd gotten familiar. The room wanted to see whether the stray understood he'd been put in his place.

"You're right," Jianxin said pleasantly. "I don't have a house, or a name anyone here would know. I came off a road. You've placed me exactly." He let that sit a moment. "It's good to be seen clearly. Most people get it wrong."

The man had expected a flush, or a stammer, or the silence of someone who knew his betters. He hadn't expected agreement, and agreement left him holding a raised hand with nothing to bring it down on.

"A man should know his station," he said, recovering.

"He should. You clearly do." Jianxin smiled. "It must be a great comfort, having yours handed to you. Saves all the trouble of earning one."

A few heads turned. The man's color changed.

"I traveled here," he said, "in the service of a house four generations your better, to assess whether the match is suitable. I would mind your tongue."

"Of course. You're the one they send to do the looking." Jianxin nodded, warm, as though conceding a fine point. "Every house needs a good pair of eyes. It's honest work, looking, for a man not suited to the other kinds."

The man took a step closer. The pleasantness had drained out of him and what was underneath it was uglier and more honest. Jianxin watched it surface and felt the read settle into place. A small man, given a small power by proximity to a large one, sent to a declining house so that for one evening he could be the one doing the measuring. The whole of him organized around the rare pleasure of looking down.

Jianxin turned his head, unhurried, and found Edric across the floor.

"Lord Edric," he called, polite, carrying. "Your man's gotten off his lead. You might want to call him back before he embarrasses your house in someone else's hall."

The room heard it. It was a clean stroke and it landed clean, and the worst of it was that it asked Edric to do something, which meant everyone now watched to see whether he would.

Edric looked over with mild interest, the expression of a man watching weather through a window. He did not move to intervene. After a moment he looked away again, back toward whatever had held his attention before, and the not-answering was so complete that a few people shifted, uncomfortable, having watched a man decline to defend his own standing without seeming to know it was his.

The Edrath man saw it too. Saw that no one was coming. And something in him, denied the backing he'd assumed was behind him, abandoned the last of the propriety he'd been performing all night and reached for the one thing that would wound.

He looked at where Lira had been standing.

"And you," he said. "A grown man, no house, no name, sweating over a girl of twelve on a dance floor. One does wonder what a stray really wants, getting his hands on the young daughter of a house he's attached himself to. Whether the dancing is all he's—"

Jianxin's hand was already moving.

It came up empty, the arm swinging through a draw that had no sword in it, the old motion his body made before his mind had finished deciding anything. And into the moving hand, between one instant and the next, a blade arrived. He did not see Tianyi do it. No one did. There was simply no sword and then there was a sword, materialized into his brother's grip mid-swing as though it had been waiting in the air for the hand to arrive.

The edge stopped against the man's throat. A hair from the skin. Not a tremor in it.

The room had gone entirely silent.

The man's eyes dropped to the thing at his neck, and what he saw made no sense to him. It was not a sword. Not really. It was stone, raw grey stone in the rough shape of a blade, and only along the cutting edge did it change, a thin seam of metal drawn out of the rock and laid down sharp, a hand's breadth of true edge on a length of mountain. The grip was wrong, unfinished, a column of the same stone that had never been shaped for a hand because there had been no time to shape it. It had been made between one breath and the next, pulled whole out of the floor and the walls and the iron fittings of the hall and pressed into being in the half-second a man's arm took to swing. The edge against his throat, though, the edge was perfect. Whatever else had been done in haste, the part that mattered had not.

He understood, dimly, that he was being held at the throat by something that had not existed a moment ago and had been built, in the time it took to want it, by someone in the room who was not even holding it.

---

The man's guard moved fast, a heavy figure shouldering through the frozen guests, hand going to his weapon, the air around him thickening as he pulled on whatever the Witness had given him.

"Take the blade off him," the guard said, "or I'll put you down where you stand."

Jianxin didn't take the blade off him. He didn't look at the guard at all. He kept his eyes on the man whose throat he was holding, and he read the guard without deciding to.

It was nothing. An empty suit. A career of standing behind someone important, of being large in rooms where largeness was enough, of a competence that had never once been tested against anyone who could actually fight. The guard's intent came to Jianxin already finished, every move the man would make laid out before he made it, each habit and tell and trained reflex a single thread, and the threads ran together into one rope, and Jianxin had the whole of it in his hand before the guard had taken his next breath.

The guard drew and struck for Jianxin's free shoulder, a clean overhand meant to drop him.

Jianxin's free hand was already where the blade was going. Not blocking. Meeting it at the one point along its path where the flat could be turned, a touch, two fingers, and the cut slid wide of him and into the air. The guard felt the sword go where he had not sent it and corrected, reversing for the ribs, and the correction had been in the rope too, and Jianxin's fingers were waiting at the new line before the edge arrived, guiding it past, the whole of it looking less like defense than like a man helping a clumsy partner through a dance he hadn't learned.

The guard pulled the blade back and put his weight behind it and drove straight for the center of him, the simple killing thrust, no finesse left in it.

Jianxin caught the blade between two fingers a hand's width from his chest. It stopped. Not because he was strong. Because his fingers were exactly where the point was going to be, and a blade with nowhere to go does not go anywhere.

The guard tried to wrench the blade free and found that he couldn't, that wherever he pulled the fingers were already there, that the steel moved as though it had agreed to belong to someone else. Something in his face came apart. He reached for the last thing he had and began to pour his power into the blade, the Witness's gift, everything he'd been given, the air screaming thin around the steel.

"Don't," Jianxin said.

He said it as you'd tell a child not to touch a stove. And the guard, who an instant ago had been a large dangerous man in a room full of his betters, stopped, because the boy holding his sword between two fingers had not raised his voice and had not looked at him and had not, in any moment of the whole exchange, appeared to be in any difficulty at all, and the animal underneath the cultivation understood before the man did that whatever this was, the power he was about to spend would buy him nothing but a clearer view of how far out of his depth he stood.

The thinning air subsided. The guard let go of the hilt. The sword hung in Jianxin's two fingers a moment, and then Jianxin let it fall, and it rang on the floor in a silent hall.

"That's better," Jianxin said, to the man at his swordpoint, as though the guard had never spoken at all.

---

The man had been under Jianxin's eyes the whole time. He understood that now, in the quiet. The guard had been turned aside without a glance, brushed off like lint, and all the while the boy had been looking at him, only him, and only now that there was nothing else in the room did he feel the full weight of it.

Not anger. The boy was reading him, going all the way down and finding him exactly as small as he was.

A bead of blood welled at his throat where the edge rested, swelled, and ran a thin line down to his collar.

He had not felt the blade move. It hadn't. He had swallowed.

Neither of them said anything. The man did not dare. He stood with the edge at his neck and discovered that the untouchable thing he stood behind was very far away and the blade was very close.

"Enough."

Lord Aldric's voice came across the hall, and Jianxin took the blade away. He stepped back, and the stone came apart in his hand, the edge and the haft losing their shape all at once and running back to grit and dust that scattered across the floor at his feet, and he stood with his arms loose and his face open as though nothing had happened at all.

Lord Aldric crossed the floor. He looked at the Edrath man, and at Jianxin, and at the guard with his hand still on a weapon he hadn't drawn, and the calculation moved behind his face in real time. He could not punish the boy. Everyone in the hall had heard what the man was reaching for when the blade arrived, and there was no version of the Arbiter's necessity in which a grown man saying that about a child of the house could be the injured party. The law was the law. The boy had been within his rights.

"You will not raise steel in my hall again," Lord Aldric said to Jianxin, because something had to be said, and that was the thing that cost him nothing to say.

"I won't need to," Jianxin said.

Casver stepped in then, into the space the reprimand had opened, his voice pitched low and grave and carrying just far enough.

"Brother, this is precisely the difficulty." He did not look at Jianxin. He looked at the vassals still close enough to hear, and grieved, which was a more useful thing to do than argue. "We take in strays off the road, and the strays draw steel on the honored guests of the house we are trying to join ourselves to. I say nothing against the boy's courage. I say only that the rest of the region is watching how we keep our hall, and tonight they have seen us fail to keep it."

Orath's mouth tightened. The younger vassal glanced toward Lord Aldric to see how he would answer and found no answer ready. That it had been earned would not survive the retelling, and Casver was already shaping it, sorrowfully, in real time, while the wound was open and the room was unsure of itself.

"A house that cannot govern its own people," Casver said, gentle, almost mournful, "can hardly ask another house to—"

Maren looked at him.

Jianxin happened to be watching her face when she did it, and for one instant the thing he saw there stopped him cold. It was not anger. Anger he could have read. It was the same thing he had felt in the family court, the morning the blood fell on the brass and the scale tipped to the side of the law. The Arbiter. The god this house had given itself to, that this whole city knelt to, the one who weighs and judges and whose verdict ends the matter. He had felt it move through the room then, in the ritual, at a distance. Now it was looking out through a woman's eyes at her own uncle, and in them, for the space of a breath, were the scales themselves, level and patient and already settled. Not a thing Maren was doing. A thing that was simply true about her, surfacing. For that instant Jianxin was not being looked at by Maren. He was being looked at by the god her family served, and it had measured Casver completely and found the matter closed.

Casver closed his mouth.

The weight passed. Maren turned back to her father. Casver did not finish his thought, and would not, and Jianxin understood that he had just watched the real arrangement of power in this family show itself for exactly as long as it took to settle a question and no longer.

"This event is concluded," Lord Aldric said. "My family thanks its guests."

---

The man at the center of it had gone gray, but the fear was already curdling into something else, because he had remembered what he was standing behind.

"You can't touch me," he said, before the room could disperse. His voice climbed. "Any of you. You understand that. I am of House Edrath. Elder Sevran's shelter is over me, and there is not a house in this region that would let one of its strays put a hand on a man under an Apostle's protection." He was looking at Lord Aldric now. "Account for your servant, or the match dies tonight, and I will tell my house precisely what I was made to stand through in yours."

The threat worked as threats worked in this room. It was true, and everyone knew it was true, and the truth of it settled over the hall and made the blade that had just been at his throat retroactively meaningless, a gesture that could never have been completed.

Lord Aldric's jaw set, but he said nothing, because there was nothing the law would let him say.

The guests gathered themselves. The evening folded up around the wound in it.

---

The brothers were sent to their rooms. It was framed as discipline and everyone understood it was not.

What happened to the Edrath man was framed as nothing and everyone understood it was discipline. His own delegation took him in hand before the Vaynes had to. Whatever he had come to assess, he had crossed a line his own house could not stand behind, because the Witness ran on propriety and there was nothing proper in what he'd said, and a house that traded on its name could not be seen to shelter that. The shelter held, technically. He would not be touched. But he left the hall early and quietly and without ceremony, in the manner of a man being removed, and both houses let it be seen that he was being removed, and that was the punishment. A slap on the wrist dressed as decorum. He would suffer nothing lasting. The alliance was worth more than his dignity, and so his dignity was what they spent.

Lira watched him go.

She had been kept to the side through all of it, a hand on her shoulder, but she had seen the whole thing. She had seen the blade come from nowhere and stop exactly where it meant to. She had seen the man who'd made her step behind her sister walk out of the hall with his head down. And she had heard, before any of it, the thing Jianxin said on the dance floor, the promise about the blade and who it belonged to, and now she had watched it be true.

She looked at Jianxin as he was led out, and he caught the look, and there was something in it that had not been there before the evening. The helplessness was still in her. He'd seen it land earlier, the suffocation, the hands of the house closing over her without asking. But it had something beside it now. She had seen that the walls could be cut. That someone would swing. That the swinging was real and not a story he'd told her to be kind.

Both things sat in her at once, and he knew which one the house would try to grow and which one it would try to starve, and he hoped that she'd remember the right one when she was old enough to use it.

---

"How hard would it be," Jianxin said, "for the two of us to kill an Apostle."

They were in their room, the lamp low, the door shut on the evening. Jianxin had his own sword out, and he was moving through forms in the cleared space between the beds, slow and unhurried, working the evening out of his arms one cut at a time. Tianyi sat properly on the bed, because Tianyi sat properly.

"That's the question you came away with."

"He said we couldn't touch him. He was right, and it annoyed me, and now I want to know how right." A cut, a turn, the blade settling. "So. The old man at the estate. If it came to it."

Tianyi set the question down a moment.

"You felt what their guard pulled up tonight," he said. "When he reached for his power."

"A little. It went nowhere." Another cut. The sword moved easy in his hand now, the tension bleeding off into the motion.

"That's a Devotee. The lowest of the three grades these people have. They give themselves to a god, all the way, in what they believe and how they live, and the god gives something back. A Devotee gets a steadiness, a competence, but only on their own god's ground. Off it, it thins to almost nothing. That man was on Vayne soil, Arbiter soil, with his Witness running thin, which is half of why he folded." He turned the lamp down a fraction. "Set against my own road, a Devotee sits about where I am now. Qi Gathering, the first stretch, the nine levels you start with before anything that has a grander name. I'm four levels into it."

"He didn't feel like much."

"He wasn't. A strong one, late into Qi Gathering, the two of us could kill, but only together, and only if it went well. One of us alone would lose. That's the floor. That's the level we're actually at, tonight, whatever it looked like in the hall."

"And the next one up."

"The Herald. Rare. The god has marked them specifically, answered them, given one real power that travels with them anywhere they go. A Herald can sit as an elder, hold land, advise a lord. The Vaynes fielded eight or nine at their height and called themselves a great house for it." He paused. "Foundation Establishment. The stage past the nine levels, the next thing up the road from where I stand. One whole step above me. And here's the part worth understanding. Even a weak one, fresh into Foundation, would beat the two of us together. We'd land a few real hits. We might make it cost something. We would still lose, and we would not get to choose whether we walked away from it." He let that sit. "Past the early part of Foundation, we don't land the hits anymore. We just lose."

"And the old man's past that."

"The old man is a long way past that. The Apostle is chosen directly. Not marked. Chosen. Given a power that bends the world around it, and there may be five of them alive in the whole world." He paused, doing the arithmetic he always did. "An Apostle stands where a Golden Core stands. The stage past Foundation, two full steps above me. And against a Golden Core, the two of us don't last a single exchange. Not lose slowly. Not get hit and keep standing. One blow, and there is no second one, and running isn't on the table because you don't outrun a thing operating at that level." He looked at Jianxin. "If we met the old man as we are tonight, we would be dead before either of us understood that anything had begun."

Jianxin's sword slowed. "So no."

"So no. Not tonight, not in any number of tonights that I can see from here. The blade on his man's throat was real. A blade on the old man's throat is a thing you'd never get close enough to put there, and if you did, it wouldn't matter." Tianyi looked at him. "That's why the threat worked. It wasn't a bluff. There is genuinely nothing the two of us could do to a house with an Apostle in it. That's what the marriage is buying. A thing that cannot be touched."

"And paying with her."

Tianyi didn't answer that, which from Tianyi was a kind of answer.

Jianxin stopped moving. He stood with the sword loose at his side and thought about it, the Golden Core, the power a god reached down and placed in a man, the kind of strength that held up a whole house without the house ever having to lift anything. And under that he thought about what Tianyi had said in passing and not dwelt on, that the gods gave the power, that all of it ran back to them, the Apostle and the Herald and the Devotee and the steadiness in the soil of every territory he'd crossed, all of it flowing out from somewhere.

"One day," he said, "I'd like to be able to touch a thing like that."

"One day," Tianyi said, "you might." He was quiet a moment. "There will be consequences in the morning. For tonight. Not from the Vaynes — they can't move against you without admitting the insult warranted it. But Casver will find a use for it, and Maren will spend the morning containing whatever he does."

Jianxin kept moving through the form. "I know."

"You don't regret it."

"No."

"Neither do I," he said, and left it there.

The lamp burned low. Tianyi set the records aside and lay down, and after a while his breathing went even, and the room was quiet.

Jianxin stayed up a while longer with the sword.

He moved through one more form, slow, the last of the evening finally gone out of his arms, and at the end of it he let the blade come to rest where it wanted to rest. It did not point at the wall, or the door, or anything in the room. It drifted off true as it settled and came to bear past all of it, the point lifting slightly, following the slope of the land itself, inward and up, toward the one direction the room did not contain.

He didn't know what was up there. He only knew the sword had chosen it without him, steady, repeatable, the blade returning to the same bearing each time he let it go. Somewhere along that line was the thing all of this came from. The grades and the soil and the old man's untouchable strength and the marriage and the girl paying for it, all of it fed from a single source he could feel the direction of and could not yet see.

The blade held, very still, pointed at the gods.
