# Chapter 8

Jianxin's room took five minutes to pack. His sword went on his back first, then the Garson manual, then the three storybooks he hadn't finished with yet, then clothes. He looked around at what remained, the desk where the candle had burned down to nothing seven years ago, the cot, the shelf of assigned texts he'd read and wouldn't need again, and felt very little about leaving them. They were furniture. The things that mattered were already in his bag.

He heard Tianyi moving in the next room, the methodical sounds of someone who had thought about this in advance. Which Tianyi had, probably. He'd likely made a list.

Jianxin sat on the edge of the cot and waited.

After a few minutes Tianyi appeared in the doorway, his bag over one shoulder, a small sealed package in his other hand that hadn't been there yesterday. The clan's delivery, arrived overnight without anyone delivering it. A cultivation manual, probably, and whatever information they'd decided he needed. Tianyi set it on the desk without opening it and looked at Jianxin.

"You know you don't have to come," he said.

Jianxin looked at him. "Good morning to you too."

"I mean it."

"Come where? We're leaving the institute. We're both leaving the institute. That's what's happening this morning."

"You know what I mean."

"I really don't." Jianxin tilted his head. "Why don't you explain it to me."

Tianyi looked at him with the patience of someone who has decided not to be played. "You figured it out. Probably years ago. That your path isn't bound by the same restriction mine is, that the outside Dao doesn't threaten you the way it threatens me, that you could go directly to the clan's territory or anywhere else and it wouldn't matter." He said it without inflection. "I've known for a while that you knew. So stop pretending you don't."

Jianxin held the look for another moment, then let it go. "Around year four," he said. "Give or take."

Something in Tianyi's posture shifted, barely perceptible. "And."

"And what?"

"What did you decide."

Jianxin looked at him for a moment. Then he stood up and picked up his bag. "Come on, we're going to be late."

"Jianxin."

"I didn't decide anything." He said it simply, without drama. "There wasn't a decision. You're my brother. Where you go I go, that's just what's true. The clan can have opinions about it."

Tianyi was quiet for a long moment. Outside the window the sky had begun to lighten, the deep blue shifting toward something paler. He picked up the sealed package from the desk.

"You could have gone to see our parents," he said. "Years ago."

"I know."

"That doesn't bother you."

Jianxin thought about it honestly. "It will, eventually. When I think about it too long." He shrugged. "I try not to think about it too long."

Tianyi looked at him with the attention he reserved for things he was trying to understand fully. Then he tucked the package into his bag and shouldered it.

"I'll pay you back," he said. "For this."

"You make the gruel when I get hurt. We're even."

"That's not the same."

"It's close enough." Jianxin grinned. "Come on."

They walked out together, down the corridor that had been theirs for seven years, past the common room where the Go boards sat dusty and unplayed on their shelf, through the main hall where the morning light was just beginning to come through the stained glass windows, painting its fractured colors on the stone floor one last time. Jianxin didn't stop to look at it. He'd looked at it enough.

The senior instructor was waiting in the courtyard. He had the stillness of someone who has been waiting comfortably and would continue to wait as long as necessary. In his hand was a second sealed package, smaller than the one the clan had sent Tianyi.

He held it out to Jianxin.

"A moment," he said. "Alone."

Tianyi walked on toward the gate without comment. Jianxin took the package and waited.

"You understand that you're not required to go with him," the instructor said. His voice was the same as it had always been, measured, unhurried, carrying the particular quality Jianxin had never been able to get beneath. "The clan has made arrangements. You could be with your family by the end of the month."

"I know," Jianxin said.

"Your brother's path will take years. Difficult years, most likely. The world outside this institute is not arranged for anyone's convenience."

"I know that too."

The instructor looked at him with an expression Jianxin still couldn't read. After seven years it remained as opaque as the first day, which was itself a kind of information, whatever was underneath it was not something that leaked.

"Then I have nothing else to say," the instructor said. He paused, and something shifted in his expression, small enough that Jianxin almost missed it. Almost. "Except that the Madam is pleased to see her sons support one another." Another pause, shorter than the first. "She misses you both severely. She would like very much to speak with you. When you're able."

He said the last part the way someone says something they mean, which was the first time in seven years Jianxin had been able to get anything from him at all. He filed it away carefully, in a place he'd be able to find it later.

"Thank you," Jianxin said. "For all of it."

The instructor nodded once, which was more than Jianxin had expected.

He walked to the gate where Tianyi was waiting. His brother looked at the package, then at his face, and asked nothing.

They walked through together.

Behind them, the institute was gone. Not demolished, not abandoned, simply no longer present, the way a thing isn't present once it's served its purpose. The lot stood empty, the stones already absorbed back into the ground, the stained glass warriors and ancient sages dissolved into the morning air. Seven years of walls and classrooms and the common room table with its wobbly left leg, all of it returned to wherever the clan's things went when they were finished being needed.

Jianxin turned to look once, found only empty ground, and turned back to the road.

Tianyi hadn't turned at all.

The path ahead ran into forest, the trees beginning where the institute's boundary had ended, tall and indifferent and immediately present. The sky was the pale blue of early morning visible in patches through the canopy, the same starless pale it always was, the light coming through at angles that made the ground beneath the trees look like it was breathing.

"So," Jianxin said. "What do you think is going to happen?"

Tianyi considered this with the seriousness it deserved. "I don't know. That's the problem. I've been trying to model what we're walking into and I don't have enough information. The variables are too many and too unknown." He paused. "It's uncomfortable."

"Uncomfortable," Jianxin said. "That's the word you're going with."

"What word would you use."

Jianxin thought about it. The path ahead ran into the trees and out the other side, toward whatever the world had been doing while they weren't watching. Somewhere out there the slope of the terrain curled everything inward, toward the plain at the center, toward whatever kept the cycles of this world turning. He'd grown up knowing that and had never thought to look at it directly.

"Interesting," he said.

Tianyi looked at him sideways. "Of course you would."

"Someone has to." He grinned. "Come on. You said you've been ready."

"I said I've been ready to leave. I didn't say I was ready for what comes after."

"That's the most honest thing you've said all morning."

"It's barely morning."

"Still counts." Jianxin fell into step beside him, his bag light on his back, his sword familiar at his side. "We'll figure it out. We always do."

Tianyi said nothing, but his pace matched Jianxin's without adjustment, the way it always had, the way it always would.

They walked into the forest together, shoulder to shoulder, as they had always been.
