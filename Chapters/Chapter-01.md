The Void. A place where light refused to linger and sound died before it was born. There was no air, no ground, no horizon. Only an endless stretch of nothing that pressed close, cold and absolute. Even thought seemed to scatter, as if the Dao itself had turned its gaze away.

Here, existence frayed at the edges.

Yet, amidst this endless abyss, there lay a woman. Her body, withered beyond recognition, still emanated a sense of inviolable majesty. Her countenance was so noble that mortals would kneel and grovel at a mere glance.

At a closer look, one would notice the woman’s swollen belly, from which a divine aura made itself known. Its existence etched the laws of reality into the abyss, manifesting something beyond names, concepts, or words. It was supreme. It was one. It was Dao.

The brilliant light of the Dao illuminated two fetuses. One radiated a flawless aura—refined and beautiful. Still, next to its neighbor, it appeared like a firefly beside the sun. The other was the origin of the light itself. It did not bask in the brilliance; it released it. Unmoving, unshaped, and absolute. From it, all things seemed to flow.

Then the Void trembled. There was no ripple, no sign of change, just sudden movement. A fissure split the abyss, and a man stepped through. He moved with calm finality. His presence cast long shadows, even here where light held no power. Behind him followed a mortal doctor. His steps were precise and unhurried. This was a man who had reached the pinnacle of medicine, whose command over life, flesh, and qi had long since transcended reverence. His eyes held only clarity.

They came to the woman. Her body remained still. Her cultivation had been sealed. Her breath lay buried beneath silence. Yet even in this state, her presence lingered like a fading command. The doctor knelt and unrolled his instruments, each one clean, gleaming, untouched by time or death. He placed a hand on her belly. Light moved beneath his fingers.

“It worked,” he said.

The man beside him nodded, unable to hide the joy that softened his gaze. “Begin.”

The scalpel moved with absolute certainty. A small incision opened. From it, light spilled in steady waves. Pure. Blinding. Endless. The doctor reached in and drew forth the first child. The infant’s aura was complete. Its qi flowed in perfect circulation. It shimmered like jade beneath moonlight. In a lesser world, this child would be revered as a dragon among men. Even so, it was a flicker beside the radiance that remained.

The doctor reached again. The second child emerged, silent and without glow. But the Void pulled back. This one held no aura, no pulse, and no sign of life by mortal standards. Yet its presence was undeniable. This was not a child born of Dao. This was Dao, given form.

In silence, the doctor wrapped the newborns and stood. He cleaned his tools and stepped away.

“Its done.”

The man received the children.

Then, the emperor drew forth a strand of qi. Another light filled the Void. This Dao was shaped, not wild like the one before. Its every pulse carried a clear and singular intent. That intent defined its nature. It was a key.

Under the glow of the key, lines appeared along the woman’s withered form. They stretched across her body, carving patterns that defied language. Each line represented pure intent. Without sound, the qi sank into the heart of the formation.

Moments passed.

Then the woman drew breath.

It came slowly and with certainty. Her limbs uncurled. Her skin returned to fullness. Her essence surged. The seals that had bound her cultivation fell away like frost beneath sunlight. She stood.

Though her expression remained calm, her aura was scattered. Her movements were uneven, as if a seasoned athlete had been forced to command a body she no longer remembered.

Her voice emerged quiet and steady. Even in weakness, her thoughts were not of herself.

“Were there… any traces of my Dao left within him?”

The man shook his head. “No. His innate Dao physique remains untouched. Your suppression was complete.”

She closed her eyes and exhaled.

“Good. Even a trace would have undone everything.”

The man looked down at the child in his arms. Still. Silent. Absolute.

“There was no interference,” he said. “He is what he was always meant to be.”

She nodded. “Then it begins.”

She stepped forward, and he placed the children in her arms. She held them in silence.

One slept, his breath steady and warm. The other remained still, his presence subtle and strange. The air around him shifted, resisting contact. Neither child gave off light anymore. Yet to their parents, they gleamed more brightly than the macrocosms beyond.

She looked down at them both, then lifted her eyes toward the Void. Her gaze pierced the layers of the abyss. Eventually, she fixed her attention on a realm.

By the standards of realms, it was not a large one. Its shape, however, was strange. The entire world sloped inward, with raised edges surrounding a plain at its center. The mountain within it orbited no sun. Its skies held no stars. Even so, time moved. Seasons passed. Life continued. The rhythm of this world followed a single object, now resting halfway up the colossal slope.

The emperor watched her and saw the weight in her eyes. He placed a gentle hand on her shoulder.

“Darling, you know this is the only way. The Innate Dao Embryo can’t survive under the full weight of the Heavenly Dao.”

“Do you think I don’t know that, Ke’er? What worries me is the Dragonkin. That creature is close to making the leap. Not to mention, That madman. The beast, the man… all of it. And with the clan unwilling to cross into Pantheon territory…”

He sighed.

“In a era this prosperous, a world with such a thin Dao is hard to find. Even if the very thing keeping it thin may be the greatest threat to our children.”

She nodded. “Has the clan at least made sure that lunatic doesn’t pull them into the madness he’s planning.”

In a small garden, two children sparred. The scene would have been heartwarming were it not for the terrifying power behind each blow.

The twins locked in combat shared the same face, but their styles could not have been more different. On one side stood a boy surrounded by energy; flames, droplets, and gusts of wind flowed around him with flawless precision, as though the heavens themselves had descended to guide each mote of power toward its destined path. His attacks reflected his temperament. Every blade of grass, every flicker of flame became a weapon, and each action, each glance, and each gesture moved with eerie precision. A flicker of fire would catch the eye just as a blade of wind thrust toward the heart. In his black eyes, the world itself seemed to unfold as a chessboard, heaven and earth reduced to pieces in play. His name matched his nature, Tianyi.

Yet his opponent seemed to weave through the endless assault with ease. His sword moved freely, seemingly without regard for stance or form, yet within that chaos lay the practiced stability of deeply honed fundamentals. His blade, though messy at first glance, was composed of tens if not hundreds of sword arts. He would slice through an oncoming sphere of water with the resolve of an old man parting the sea, then close the distance with the grace of a duelist riding the wind. He was the raging storm and the stubborn mountain; fishermen, knights, scholars, and warriors all seemed to flow through his movements without discord. His body became a vessel through which the swordsmen of ages past once again wielded their blades. Despite his fierce strikes, his eyes as he gazed at his brother were not those of a warrior, but rather of a child enjoying play with his favorite toy. His blade screamed his name, Jianxin.

Their blades clashed again, ringing like chimes beneath the morning sun. Ripples ran through the pond, scattering reflections across the garden walls.

Jianxin broke away first, sliding back across the grass, his grin flashing. “If you’d acted just then,” he said, pointing his sword at Tianyi, “the fight would’ve been over. You had the opening, but you think too much.”

Tianyi exhaled softly, his gaze unshaken. The air around him stirred in response, a faint breeze carrying motes of light that followed the rhythm of his breath. “And yet here you stand, out of stance and breathing unevenly,” he said. “Perhaps it’s better I waited.”

Jianxin clicked his tongue, feinting a strike. “You always hide behind that calm of yours. If the heavens themselves fell, I swear you’d still be counting the clouds.”

Tianyi raised a hand and the air rippled. His tone was quiet, measured. “Counting keeps one from being crushed beneath them.”

Jianxin lunged, laughing under his breath. “Maybe, but it also keeps one from living!”

Their exchange became a blur. Jianxin’s blade darting with unrestrained energy, Tianyi’s movements smooth and inevitable, the Dao flowing around him as naturally as a river follows its course.

Jianxin’s sword swept low, forcing Tianyi back a step. “You move like the heavens themselves are holding your hand,” he said, his grin widening again. “One day you’ll have to fight without them.”

Tianyi’s eyes glimmered faintly. “And one day you’ll actually have to use your sword correctly.”

Jianxin laughed again, though his grip tightened slightly. “Hah! Then let’s both hope that day comes before that old geezer finally blows his lid.”

Tianyi simply stared at him impassively, yet the image of their combat instructor's frustrated face put a smile on his face that couldn’t be hidden from someone who knew him as well as Jianxin.

Their qi flared once more, wind and steel colliding in a symphony of motion. Jianxin darted through the currents, his sword a streak of silver that hummed with his pulse. Every swing carried his spirit; every step came with the rhythm of instinct.

Yet the harder he pressed, the heavier the air seemed to grow. Tianyi’s movements remained effortless; too effortless. He never struck first, never forced an opening, yet the world itself seemed to tilt in his favor. Fire bent to his will, wind shifted to meet his needs, and the ground answered his stance like an old companion. Jianxin felt as though he were fighting inside a dream not his own.

Each movement demanded more effort than the last. His blade met invisible resistance, as if the air around Tianyi thickened, dulling every motion.

It’s like fighting underwater, Jianxin thought, his jaw tightening. Every step I take, the current pushes me back. Every breath, every strike… it’s like the world itself refuses to let me touch him.

He grit his teeth and surged forward, ignoring the drag on his limbs. His blade came down in a fierce arc aimed at Tianyi’s shoulder. For a heartbeat, it looked perfect.

Yet even his perfect stance seemed to lack something, leaving a opportunity too big for an opponent like Tianyi. His attack shredded the shield that surrounded Tianyi, yet Tianyi moved with quiet inevitability, his hand rising as the surrounding wind caught Jianxin’s momentum and turned it aside.

Jianxin stumbled. His sword arm overextended, leaving his chest open.

Before he could recover, Tianyi’s palm struck gently against his shoulder. A pulse of force rippled outward, sending Jianxin skidding backward across the garden stones. He stopped just short of the pond’s edge, the surface trembling from the displaced air.

For a long moment, there was only the sound of rustling leaves and the faint drip of water.

Jianxin stared down at his trembling hands, his grip still tight around his sword. His breathing steadied, but his heart did not. His mind was already turning over why the strike had failed.

Across from him, Tianyi stood still, his expression unreadable. The Dao around him quieted, returning to stillness.

Jianxin exhaled slowly, lowering his blade. “One day,” he said under his breath, “I’ll drag you into the water with me.”

Tianyi gave no reply, but for an instant, a faint smile touched his lips.

The wind shifted, and the garden fell silent once more as the two brothers slowly made their way back inside. Shoulder to shoulder, as they had always been.
