
The two brothers made their way into a gray stone building that appeared no older than the children themselves, perhaps seven or eight years old. Built in the shape of a cross, it resembled the many places of worship that dotted the surrounding city, differing only in the abject lack of worship within its halls.

Instead of gods and angels, legends of mighty warriors and ancient sages were depicted across its stained glass windows. Light filtered through them, painting fractured colors upon the stone floor. Yet despite the artistry there was no altar, no incense, no prayer. At the building's center, where a cathedral might have placed its nave, there was instead a wide open space that felt more like an arena than a place of devotion.

As they made their way back to their rooms, Jianxin couldn't help but throw one last jab. "You wanna have a run at another game of Go before we get ready for classes?" A shit-eating grin spread across his face as he said it.

Tianyi didn't give him the satisfaction of a response, disappearing into his room without breaking stride.

It was a fair goad. Despite the analytical nature of games like Go, Jianxin overwhelmed Tianyi every time they played. In smaller, less complex games Tianyi still prevailed, but as the domain of the game grew larger something shifted in Jianxin, some instinct that didn't operate through calculation at all. He would place a stone in a position that looked like a mistake, and you would only understand it three moves later when it turned out to be a counter to an attack you hadn't yet conceived of making.

Jianxin made his way back to his own room, still grinning, and dropped onto his bed.

The grin faded slowly as the spar came back to him.

He lay there tracing it out with his fingers against the blanket, replaying the sequence in the particular obsessive way his mind had with things it couldn't resolve. The minutes passed and the fatigue he should have been sleeping off only seemed to build. No matter how he turned it over there was no reason his last strike should have failed. From the position of his legs to the angle of his grip, his form had been the kind you could have lifted directly into a textbook. He had done everything correctly and Tianyi had still turned it aside like it was nothing, and Jianxin could not find the flaw.

He gave up on sleep and sat up.

On his desk, beneath a set of scrolls his weapons instructor had assigned last week and which he had not yet opened, was a slim manual he'd found wedged between two other texts in the academy library. He'd taken it without much intention, drawn by the fact that it had no title and no author listed on the spine. Only on the inside cover, written in a hand so precise it looked typeset: *Garson.* Below it, a date, and nothing else.

He picked it up and flipped through it standing, the way he read things he didn't expect much from.

The first section was case notes. A merchant accused of falsifying weights. The evidence, the witnesses, the ruling. He skimmed. The second section was the same, a land dispute between three families that Garson laid out the way you'd lay out a mathematical proof, eliminating possibilities until only one remained. Jianxin found himself looking for the flaw in it, the place where the judge had cut a corner or leaned on assumption, and there wasn't one. That annoyed him more than a flaw would have.

*Cold,* he thought, dropping back onto his bed with the manual. *The man writes like he's never met a person in his life.*

He read it again anyway.

The third section was different, not in style but in subject. A case where the evidence pointed clearly to one man, but two witnesses contradicted each other on a minor point. Most judges would have either dismissed the contradiction or let it cloud the whole proceeding, but Garson had traced it to its source, a difference in vantage point rather than in honesty, and then set it aside without ignoring it. The distinction mattered to him in a way that didn't need explaining.

Jianxin sat up slightly.

He went back to the merchant case and read it again, slower. The elimination of possibilities had felt cold the first time through. Now it felt like something else. Garson wasn't being cold. He simply wasn't being anything else. There was no performance of fairness, no reaching toward a conclusion that felt right. The reasoning moved because it had nowhere else to go.

He turned back to the land dispute and tried to find the place where Garson had made a choice rather than a deduction. He read it twice before he accepted that there wasn't one.

By the fourth case he had stopped looking for flaws, though he wasn't sure when that had happened. At some point his thoughts had stopped running alongside the text and started running inside it. He was no longer reading Garson's conclusions and evaluating them. He was watching Garson think, tracing the order in which possibilities were considered, the weight given to each one, the moment each was released.

A disputed inheritance came next, five potential heirs each holding a partial claim. Garson opened not with the strongest argument but with the one most likely to create confusion if left unresolved, collapsed it cleanly, and moved on. By the time he reached the strongest claim two thirds of the complexity was already gone, and what remained resolved itself almost without comment.

Jianxin read it again. The same order, the same sequence of elimination. He felt something shift in the shape of his own attention, a narrowing that had nothing to do with the content of the cases and everything to do with the structure underneath them.

He didn't notice that his grip on the manual had loosened, or that his sword hand, usually held close out of old habit, had drifted to his lap. He didn't notice his shoulders dropping, not into relaxation but into the mild forward curl of a man who had spent his hours at a desk for so long his body had learned to expect nothing else from itself.

The last section wasn't cases. It was something closer to notes, observations Garson had written at the margins of his own work, short and unaddressed to anyone.

Jianxin read one:

*The question is never what is right. The question is what remains when everything that cannot be is removed. What remains is the answer. It was always the answer. The deliberation is only the process of seeing it.*

He read it again, then again.

His objections rose and dissolved before they could fully form. Each time he found an edge to pull at, the text had already accounted for it, not by addressing the objection directly but by having been constructed in a way that made it irrelevant. He would begin to think _but what about_ and find that three sections back Garson had quietly closed that door, and the next door, and the one after that. There was nothing to argue with. There was only the reasoning, moving forward, and the slow recognition that he had run out of places to stand outside it.

The room had gone quiet in the way rooms go quiet when you stop hearing them. His thoughts were moving in a single direction now, the way water moves when the channel narrows, not faster but without the small eddies, the backward currents. Just forward.

His posture had continued its slow drift without him. The precise alignment his instructor had spent years drilling into his spine was simply gone, not broken, just vacated. He sat with the settled, slightly collapsed weight of a man whose body had never been asked to be a weapon, his chin dipped, his breathing shallow and slow. Somewhere in the text he had become sedentary in the way that only years behind a bench produces, and he had not noticed any of it happen.

He read the note a final time.

*What remains when everything that cannot be is removed.*

His sword arm moved, not because he decided to move it but because within the structure of that thought it was simply the next thing. The strike that had failed in the garden, he could see now exactly where the flaw had lived, the opening it had left, what an opponent like Tianyi would always do with space like that. The correction wasn't a correction. It was what the thought completed into.

The blade came up slowly. The grip was wrong, too loose, too far back on the handle, the wrist angle something his instructor would have winced at. It moved with none of the fluid economy that made Jianxin's sword what it was, no instinct in it, no music.

But it arrived.

That was the only word for it. Not thrust, not strike. The blade stopped an inch from the wall with the unhurried finality of a door being closed, carrying no more force than the conclusion required. Just the last step in a sequence that had only ever been going one place.

Then the clarity broke.

It didn't collapse dramatically. It simply stopped being there, the way a word stops making sense if you repeat it too many times. One moment his thoughts were moving in that clean single channel and the next they weren't. He was aware of the room again, the weight of the manual in his other hand, the wrong angle of his wrist, the unfamiliar heaviness in his shoulders, the fact that he was sitting like a man twice his age and perhaps twice his weight as well.

He straightened slowly and felt his spine remember itself.

His hands were steady. His breathing was even. But something in him felt like a man returning from somewhere he hadn't meant to go, only now recalling what his own face looked like.

He looked at the wall where his blade had stopped. Then down at the manual.

*So that's what it is.*  The shape of someone else's mind, worn briefly like a coat cut for a different body entirely.

He rolled his shoulder and felt the familiar tension return, the trained readiness coming back like a tide.

*What remains when everything that cannot be is removed.*

He wasn't sure he believed it. But he understood now, in a way that had nothing to do with believing, exactly what it felt like to be someone who did. Jianxin slowly settled back into being himself and set the manual down on his desk.
