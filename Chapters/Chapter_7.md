# Chapter 7

The seven years went the way years go when you are too busy living inside them to watch them pass.

Jianxin read everything he could find. Not the assigned texts, though he read those too, but the ones that had no business being in an institute library, fables and folk tales, histories of people who had lived and been forgotten, records of disputes and loves and small catastrophes that had mattered enormously to the people inside them and not at all to anyone else. He read them the way he had learned to read Garson, looking past the words at the mind that had arranged them, and in the arrangement he found the edges of intent he could work with.

He built sword arts out of them. The technique of a foolish fisherman who kept casting his net in the wrong waters until one day he didn't. The guard of a woman who had written letters to someone who never wrote back, all that particular patience in her brushwork, all that contained wanting. A story about two brothers who couldn't agree on how to divide their father's land, and the movement that emerged from the specific shape of that argument, the way neither of them could quite bring themselves to win.

The sword arts were strange. The instructors watched him practice them with expressions Jianxin couldn't read, which happened more and more as the years passed. They were like trying to read water, something moved beneath the surface, something with weight and direction, but the moment he reached for it the surface closed over and he was left with nothing. After the third year he stopped trying. Whatever the instructors were, they were more than they looked, and he filed that away in the category of things to think about later.

What he could do was watch Tianyi with them, and from Tianyi he could read plenty.

Tianyi, by the fourth year, had become something remarkable even by the standards of people who had been told all their lives that he would be remarkable. He moved through domains the way he moved through the early Go games, absorbing the structure of a thing until he could generate from it, until the map drew itself forward. Alchemy arrived in a month. Formation theory in three weeks. A visiting instructor in celestial cartography stayed for six days and left looking slightly unsettled, having apparently expected to do more teaching. The clan's people came and went with their particular efficiency, and whatever they made of what they saw, they said nothing useful.

Jianxin watched his brother and felt something settle in him, not resignation exactly, more like the satisfaction of understanding something that had always been true. The domains he would never touch, the ones that required systematic comprehension, the ones that rewarded accumulation and analysis, those were Tianyi's, had always been Tianyi's, would remain so for as long as they lived. He had made peace with this sometime around the fifth year, in the way you make peace with the shape of your own hands.

What he had was the sword, and the sword was enough, because the sword had no ceiling he could see.

By sixteen Jianxin had stopped thinking of combat as something that happened between bodies and had started thinking of it as a conversation between intents. The flow of a fight moved the way a conversation moved, currents and eddies, a statement demanding a response, silence that meant something different depending on what came before it. He could feel the shape of an exchange before it resolved, the way you feel a sentence turning before the words arrive. Against the instructors in practice he lost consistently and without learning much, their intents too deep and too settled to grip. Against Tianyi he won consistently and with great interest, because Tianyi's mind had become the most complex thing available to him and he had spent seven years learning its texture.

The only irritant, and it was a small one, was that Tianyi's calculations had grown to the point where some of what Jianxin did in the early exchanges was no longer invisible. Not all of it. But enough that the margin of mystery had narrowed, and that was something worth paying attention to.

Tianyi, for his part, had stopped asking what he was missing and started asking how it worked. The distinction was subtle but Jianxin felt it. A man who asks what he's missing believes there is an answer he can find. A man who asks how it works has accepted that the answer is the mechanism, and is trying to understand the mechanism well enough to build a response to it even without replicating it. That was a more interesting problem and a more dangerous one.

The Go boards gathered dust in the common room. By unspoken agreement they hadn't played since the night of Jianxin's first win, both of them unwilling to find out something they weren't ready to know yet.

---

The morning of their sixteenth birthday the senior instructor came to their rooms before dawn.

He was the oldest of the clan's people, the one who had been there since the beginning, who had never taught them directly but whose presence had been the constant background of everything. The one who signed the supply orders, who met with the other instructors, who occasionally stood in the garden at dusk looking at the sloped horizon with an expression Jianxin had never been able to read. He knocked once and they both opened their doors at the same time, which he seemed to expect.

"It's time," he said. "Take what you need. Leave what you don't."

That was all.
