# Chapter 3

The board took up most of the common room table, the same two chairs, the same wobble in the left leg neither of them had ever bothered to fix. Tianyi set out the stones, and Jianxin watched him do it, because his brother had a night's work waiting upstairs and had come down anyway.

"Thought you didn't have the hour," Jianxin said.

"I don't." Tianyi settled the last stone into the bowl. "Some of us only get the hours we steal."

Jianxin took black and went first, as he always did, and tried to do the thing he'd been turning over since calligraphy.

He reached for Tianyi as he'd reached for Garson, looking for the mind under the moves. He'd expected his brother, of all people, to be easy.

He got nothing he didn't already have. Eight years of Tianyi, recognition rather than discovery, and recognition was no use here. There was no new angle. Just his brother, familiar as his own reflection, seen from the same side he'd always seen him.

So he stopped reaching and played.

Tianyi built his web across the middle game with the patience he brought to everything, no aggression, no overcommitment, lines of pressure across several regions at once, each one answerable alone and collectively starting to weigh. Jianxin had grown up inside that web. He knew its texture like a recurring dream. He placed a stone in the upper left that looked like territory.

It wasn't. He hadn't planned it exactly. It came from knowing his brother, from years of drowning in that web. Tianyi liked his pressure distributed, never committing to one decisive threat when he could hold five inconclusive ones. To do that his attacking lines had to converge, and there was a point in the upper left where two of his natural convergences would eventually want to meet.

The stone sat there and said nothing. Tianyi looked at it, placed his own stone elsewhere, and the game went on.

It was in the next sequence that Jianxin caught it. Tianyi was still building with the same patience, but the lines had begun to angle away from the upper left. Not dramatically. A small, consistent deflection, the kind that adds up across moves until you notice the web you thought was everywhere has a region it keeps not quite entering. Tianyi had seen the stone. Not as a threat yet, not consciously enough to answer. But somewhere in the calculation it had registered as a future problem, and the web was already bending around it.

There it was. Diffuse intention beginning to cohere around a point.

Jianxin reached again, not for the familiar shape of his brother's whole mind but for this one thing, this narrowing, and found it far easier to hold. A diffuse mind was unreadable. A mind beginning to focus had a direction, and a direction could be read.

He placed his stones to keep the balancing act alive, making sure neither of Tianyi's commitments was ever quite enough. He wasn't playing the board anymore. He was playing the mind playing the board, and the mind was legible because he'd given it something to organize around. The game closed out over the next twenty moves. Tianyi never made an obvious error. He just ran out of good responses, the web slowly smothered by the cost of the balancing act, the stone in the corner a drain that had been pulling from everything since the middle game.

Tianyi looked at the board a long time after the final stone.

"When did you place that."

"Upper left? Twelve moves before you noticed it."

He traced the game backward in his head, the slight stillness that meant full processing. "I want to learn Go properly," he said finally.

"You know Go properly."

"I want to learn it without you in it." He began collecting stones with the same efficiency he'd set them out. "There's something in the way you play that I can't account for yet. I'd rather understand the game first and account for you separately."

He said it without heat. To him it was only an observation. And Jianxin, watching him gather the stones, understood the thing under it that his brother would never name. Tianyi could do that. He could leave, go and get the answer, study the game to its floor and come back with the gap closed, because the world had always handed his brother a road to walk when a thing stood in his way. Whatever Jianxin couldn't do, no one was going to teach him. He'd have to make the answer himself, out of nothing, in the corner of a school that was about someone else.

"It'll still be there when you come back," Jianxin said. "The thing you can't account for."

"Maybe." Tianyi set the last stone in its bowl. "Or I'll have closed the gap enough that it won't matter."

He left without waiting for an answer.

---

The arena was empty at dawn, the stained glass throwing its fractured colors across the floor with no one to receive them. Jianxin had come early on purpose. He liked the space before Tianyi arrived, when it was still possible to hear the building breathe.

Tianyi came exactly when he'd said, which was always. They didn't speak much before a spar. Jianxin rolled his shoulders. Tianyi settled into his stance and the air around him shifted, a change in pressure that made the room feel larger and less forgiving.

Tianyi opened with fire, a feint toward the left that was never meant to land, drawing the eye while a current of wind came low along the ground. Jianxin read the fire for what it was, stepped clear of the wind, and drove into the gap it left, his blade coming through fast and low, an old swordsman's cut he'd absorbed so thoroughly it no longer felt borrowed, and Tianyi had to give a full step sideways to avoid it.

That was new. Tianyi noted it.

They reset. The web built as it always did, patient and spread, fire drawing attention here while water pressured the footing there, earth anchoring his center, wind moving through the gaps in Jianxin's guard. None of it decisive alone. All of it together made the arena feel like it was slowly tilting.

But Jianxin had come to practice the other thing. He stopped looking for a way through the web and started looking for a way to make Tianyi watch one door long enough to stop watching the others. He came in hard on the left, a feint he committed to just enough to believe, and when Tianyi's fire moved to cut the angle, Jianxin shifted his weight and let his blade present a gap on the right, a fraction of an opening that would have rewarded a faster commitment.

Tianyi didn't take it. He was too precise. But something in the web's shape shifted around that angle. He had seen it. He'd registered it as a possibility, and the registration cost him a fraction of the attention he'd been spending on the water pressure on the left.

There it was again. The same narrowing as the stone in the upper left. A mind beginning to organize around a fixed point, and a fixed point was legible in a way distributed pressure never was.

The exchange that followed was the best they'd had, faster than the garden, both of them more serious than the morning called for. Jianxin kept the gap on the right alive, an unresolved threat Tianyi now had to account for with every move. He could feel the web tightening around it as the Go pattern had bent around the corner stone, the suffocating distributed pressure of his brother's style starting to converge.

Tianyi's lightning came once, singular and decisive, aimed at the instant Jianxin's footwork crossed. Jianxin read it early enough to clear it fully, and didn't, letting it catch his side instead of his center because he wanted to know how committed Tianyi was willing to get. The force radiated through his ribs and stole a breath. Committed enough.

He stepped back and took the breath, and Tianyi let him, which was generous and a little condescending.

"You're compensating on the left," Jianxin said. "Your water comes earlier than it used to when I pressure that side."

A pause. "You've been counting."

"I've been reading." He raised the sword. "Come on."

He pushed the left, watched the right thin. Pushed the right, watched the left thin. He did it three more times, learning the rhythm of how Tianyi's attention moved between the two threats, not as calculation, as a shape he could feel the edges of. Then he set up the shoulder. He let Tianyi land the hit he'd been baiting, moved into it instead of away, felt the sharpness spread down his arm and his guard on the right drop exactly as far as it was going to drop. He'd been building to this across the whole spar. The record was complete. The evidence sufficient.

He stopped thinking about it.

The clarity came faster than it had with Garson's manual, compressed into the weight of everything that had landed on him. The forearm. The ribs. The shoulder. What remained when everything that could not be was removed was a single line from where he stood to where Tianyi's guard had opened.

His blade moved. It had none of the music of his usual sword, the grip too deliberate, the weight behind it conclusion and not instinct. It arrived at Tianyi's guard with the unhurried finality of a verdict, carrying exactly the force required. The guard gave. The blade came to rest at his brother's throat.

The arena was quiet. The colored light lay still on the floor between them.

Then the state broke, and the shoulder, the ribs, and the forearm all arrived at once, and the sound that came out of Jianxin was not one he'd planned.

Tianyi's eyes moved from the blade to his brother's face. "How bad." He didn't wait for the answer before his hand was at Jianxin's side. "You took that hit on purpose."

"Brilliant tactical decision-making."

"You're an idiot." He got under Jianxin's good arm without discussion. "Infirmary."

---

The infirmary was a single room at the back, two cots and a cabinet of medicinal supplies the clan restocked every few months without anyone coming to restock it. Things appeared when they were needed and disappeared when they weren't, and asking where they came from had never produced a satisfying answer.

Most of what appeared was for Tianyi. The rare medicines, the texts that came sealed, the cultivation aids shaped to a physique only one of them had, those arrived with a care the rest of the stores never got. Jianxin's share was the general stock, the things any body could use. He'd worked it out years ago and made his peace with it.

Tianyi heated water with a touch of fire qi so controlled it was almost domestic, and put the medicinal gruel together with the same precision as everything, the herbs measured and added in order, the heat kept low. It tasted, when it came, like concentrated effort and vaguely of ginger, and worked better than it had any right to given how unpleasant it was to eat.

"It smells wrong," Jianxin said.

"It's supposed to smell wrong. That's how you know it's working." Tianyi set the bowl down. "Eat it."

He ate it. After a few minutes the qi began to move through the bruised tissue with a warmth that meant it was doing its work, and he set the bowl down and tested the arm. Better. Still sore.

"The shoulder hit," Jianxin said. "I set it up over the whole spar. Baited the angle on the right from the third exchange, kept it open long enough that you had to account for it, walked into the water to give you the forearm so the pattern would lock, and by the time the shoulder came you'd already decided what I'd do next."

"I understood most of it during. Not all." Tianyi considered. "Something changed in your sword at the end. The movement was wrong for your style. I kept trying to find it in your footwork and it wasn't there."

That was worth knowing, that it read as stylistically foreign and not as his own method pushed somewhere new. "The problem is I can't do it again. Not reliably. The way in needs time to build, and I don't understand it well enough to shorten the road. Against someone faster than you, that cost would kill me." He turned the empty bowl in his hands. "I beat you with a thing I can't repeat and can't explain. That's not a victory. That's an accident I happened to survive."

Tianyi didn't argue, because it was true. "Then you find a way in with less setup."

"Or make the setup cheaper." Jianxin set the bowl down. "First time I've beaten you, by the way. At something you're supposed to be better at."

Tianyi said nothing, enduring what he'd already accepted as inevitable.

"I'm going to remember this for a very long time."

"I know."

Outside the single window the evening had gone fully dark, the sky the deep starless blue it always was on this world. They both knew what the years between now and leaving were for. The Dao out there was dense and old, and Tianyi's physique had to become its own thing first, rooted enough that the outside couldn't simply write over it. That was why they were here instead of there, why the clan had built this whole place around his brother and left the two of them in it.

Jianxin had done the arithmetic of it long ago. The institute was here because of Tianyi. The instructors were here because of Tianyi. The years they'd both spent inside these walls were years the world had to wait while his brother grew solid enough to enter it. He was here, in the most literal sense, because his brother needed him to be, a life arranged start to finish around a physique that wasn't his.

He'd expected, when he first understood this, to mind it more than he did. What he'd found instead was the opposite. Nothing in this place had been built for him. No path laid down, no method calibrated, no instructor sent to make him into anything in particular. Tianyi had a road, a real one, measured and waiting, every step of it known before he took it. Jianxin had been left to his own devices in the corner of a school that was about someone else, and somewhere in that neglect he'd found the one thing that was entirely his. He'd had to make it himself, out of whatever he was, because no one had thought to make it for him.

The chosen son got the road. He got to find out where his own feet wanted to go.

"Get some sleep," Tianyi said. "Your shoulder needs it."

Jianxin lay back on the cot, careful of the shoulder, and looked at the ceiling, and thought about the world out there that he hadn't read yet. Cities. The crowds, the weight of a thousand lives pressing against each other. He'd built sword arts out of people in books. Out there were people he'd never read, intents he'd never felt, the full unmanageable texture of a world that had been doing things without him since before he was born.

Seven years until he could walk into it. He found he resented every one of them.
