# Chapter 7

The escorts got the carriage back on its wheels while Tianyi got Jianxin out of his shirt.

The cut above the hip was deep and clean, and it had stopped being warm and started being painful by the time Tianyi had him sitting on the carriage step, the wound packed, Jianxin's own thumb holding the binding while his brother wound it tight.

"You won't swing for a week," Tianyi said.

"I'll swing in three days."

"You'll tear it open in three days, and I'll close it again, and we'll do this twice." He tied it off. Whatever else Tianyi was careless about, it was never this. "It went in where you couldn't read."

"It went in where there was nothing to read." Down the road the dead lay in the shapes the fight had left them, Maren's people already stepping around them like furniture. "Thirty-one of them I could've told you the insides of. The thirty-second wasn't a man. It was the space between six of them, and it's the only thing on this whole road that put a blade in me." He prodded the binding, winced, and grinned through it anyway. "I'd like to learn that."

"Of course you would."

They were given the luggage carriage, and the luggage found other homes without anyone asking the brothers to lift it. By the time the light began to slant the road was clear, and the column went northeast toward Aldenmere as though thirty-two men had never come out of the treeline for it an hour before.

He spent that first day on the carriage roof, tracing the bandits' blades back through the fight one stroke at a time. He'd caught the gap between what a man's body says and what his blade does before, in Garson's notes, in sparring with Tianyi, but he'd always had to reach for it. On the road it had simply been there, plain as the surface itself. Something had opened.

What he could not do was read the formation. When the six had come together he'd lost the individual men, not because they vanished but because something ran between them, a coordination that lived in no single body but in the space between bodies, and when he reached for it there was no one in it to take hold of.

He turned it over the whole first day and got no nearer. He could read what was inside a person to the floor. He could not read what was between them. And on a road, in a fight, the thing that had nearly killed him was the between, not the men. It sat in him like a splinter, the first wall his gift had hit that he could not just push harder against, and he could not leave it alone. Whatever the rest of this place turned out to be, he wanted to learn to read the space between people, and he had no idea where a person even started on a thing like that.

"Tianyi." His brother was walking beside the carriage, reading. "How many merchant convoys did we pass on the road from the forest?"

"Eleven. Three more on the tributary near the second hill."

Jianxin looked at the wagons ahead, the goods, the few guards, the gap between what they carried and how easy it would be to take. Then at Maren's carriage, the two trained escorts, the thirty-two men someone had sent. "Someone sent them."

Tianyi had already closed his book. "Yes."

Neither of them said anything further.

---

The road to Aldenmere took three days, and the nearer it came the more the world filled in, until the road was never empty for long. Jianxin was rarely on the carriage. By the first night he was at a mercenary company's fire trading stories as though he'd ridden with them for years; the next day he'd talked his way into a passing merchant train's supper and come back with their names, their cargo, and a warning about a high pass that shut the moment the rock crested, a man who tried it between being a man the road kept. He turned it on everyone, because everyone was new, and it worked faster than it had any right to.

But every fire he sat at, he was also listening for the thing he couldn't do. He'd watch two men who'd traveled together for years pass a whole argument between them in three words and a look, and reach for what ran in the gap, and close on nothing. The men he could read. The thing between them stayed shut to him. He marked each failure and kept sitting down at fires.

Maren watched him work the road and said nothing, and added it to whatever she was building. He watched her back. She had a way of organizing the space around her, each sentence to the escorts doing one thing and stopping, and when she looked at the carriage she was counting something, damage, the hours of light left. He reached for what sat under it and found no gap there at all. She had simply decided what she thought before the situation finished presenting itself, and everything after was execution. He'd felt it once before, in a dead man's case notes, frictionless, moving because it had nowhere else to go. He set it aside and kept watching.

On the second day she fell into step beside him, after he'd left a carter laughing who'd been cursing a broken axle when he walked up. "How do you do that," she said. "Most people would have waited for him to finish being angry."

He gave her his brother's flattest voice. "That seems inefficient." Then he grinned and dropped it. "Mostly I just like people better when they're laughing."

She looked at him a moment, then went back to the carriage without answering, and he watched her go and found her more interesting than he had the day before.

The checkpoint came out of the road's curve, guards in formal livery, a gate structure that meant more than a toll. The carriage slowed and Maren gave her name, and the guard's posture adjusted. They were waved through, then stopped.

"Names?"

"Jianxin."

The guard waited. "That's it," Jianxin said.

"Tianyi," said Tianyi, when the guard looked at him.

"Family?"

"We don't have one to give," Tianyi said.

The guard wrote something, crossed it out, wrote something else, and waved them through.

It was at the gate that Jianxin felt the air change.

Not the temperature. Something under it. A weight that settled onto his chest and the back of his neck as they passed beneath the structure, an orderliness that wasn't in the architecture, a sense of having crossed from ground that belonged to no one onto ground that belonged, wholly, to something. It thinned when he stopped attending to it and never fully left. He looked at Tianyi and saw his brother had felt it too, had gone still with it, cataloguing.

"You feel that," Jianxin said.

"Yes. It started at the gate, not before." Tianyi looked back at the structure. "It's in the ground. Not the air. We crossed into something."

---

Aldenmere opened ahead of them, and it arranged itself by rank as it climbed the slope. Noble quarters on the high side, where the ground rose toward the distant mountain at the world's heart, the stone kept clean because it mattered who saw it. Merchant tier below, denser, commerce in every storefront and dock. Lower districts at the base, where the ground fell toward the rim, the streets narrow and the buildings old.

At a junction a shrine stood against a building's corner, three stone figures, weathered, attended, fresh offerings small and practical at the base. One figure held a scale. One held a flame. The third Jianxin couldn't make out at the carriage's speed, and found he wanted to, and couldn't say why the wanting had an edge of unease on it.

Further up, where the merchant tier met the noble, a crowd had gathered around a low platform, perhaps thirty people at a respectful distance. A man knelt with his hands bound. In front of him stood another in dark robes, a scale embroidered at the breast in silver thread. The crowd watched without sound. No one pushed for a better view. No one looked away. The robed man said something, the kneeling man nodded once, his head already lowered, and the carriage moved on before it finished. Jianxin looked back. The crowd was already dispersing.

On the noble tier a church dominated a plaza, cross-shaped, large, the same architectural bones as the institute grown to full size and made for use. Three figures in the stained glass above the entrance. Not the warriors and sages he'd grown up with.

Below the church two men had stopped in the road to argue, one dressed for the merchant tier and one for the tier under it. A man in noble livery passed between them on his way to somewhere else, not slowing, not looking, and both of them stepped back to let him through, unasked, and did not resume once he was gone. The rank had answered the thing they were arguing about, and no one had had to say so. Jianxin watched it in the mirror of the carriage and could not decide whether it was the most orderly thing he had ever seen or the most frightening.

And under all of it, the weight, lower than the air, thickest around the shrine and the platform and the cross-shaped church, thinning between them, never quite gone. He had no name for it. He noticed that it gathered where the three figures were, and that there were three of them everywhere he looked, and that they did not seem to sit easily together, the scale and the flame and the third he hadn't seen, as though the city served more than one thing and the things did not entirely agree. He let it be, though it would not quite let him be.

---

The Vayne estate sat at the upper tier's edge, significant but not the highest, the stonework clean, nothing out of place. Lord Aldric received them in a formal room on the second floor. He had made them come to him.

He was broad, grey at the temples, and he had gathered his children to receive the two strangers Maren had brought in off the road. Aldren stood at his shoulder, the eldest, built like his father but without the settled weight, a posture worn in by enough repetition that it had stopped requiring effort and started requiring maintenance: the heir, and never once unaware of it. Cael, the younger son, leaned against the wall with his arms crossed and watched the brothers from the moment they came in, with the attention of someone who'd been waiting for something interesting to happen. Near the window, on a low chair, sat the youngest, a girl of eleven or twelve with Maren's eyes and none of Maren's guard, who made no pretense of looking anywhere but at them. Lira, though no one had said so yet.

Lord Aldric looked at the brothers and took his time. Then he let them feel him.

It was nothing he did with his face or his hands. The weight Jianxin had carried at the edge of his attention since the gate, spread thin across the whole city, drew in and settled over the two of them, and gathered into one man it was not thin at all. It pressed on the chest and the back of the neck. The air turned dense and attentive.

His own answered before he could weigh it, his weight dropping, his hand settling where the sword was, and beside him Tianyi had gone still, the room become a set of lines with an exit at the end of them. Neither of them had decided to.

"Don't." Maren did not raise her voice. "My father is a Herald. A hair's breadth from an Apostle, and has been for years." She looked at the brothers, not at the man testing them. "Two men at a Devoted's measure wouldn't finish drawing the blade."

The weight drew back, slow, into the man it had come from. Lord Aldric had not moved through any of it. Whatever he'd wanted to know, the speed of the answer had given it to him.

Jianxin filed the words he didn't have yet. Herald. Apostle. Devoted. And the thing under them, that whatever he and Tianyi were, they'd been placed at the bottom of it, measured by a woman who'd thrown the fact down flat to keep them from dying of their own reflexes. He'd walked in the most dangerous man he'd ever stood near, and the man was a Herald, and a Herald was a hair from a thing called an Apostle, and there was a whole ladder above that he couldn't see the top of. He'd thought, on the road, that breaking thirty-two men meant something. It had bought him a place at the bottom of a stairwell he hadn't known was there.

"They came off the mountain," Maren said, before the quiet could set. "Den country, by the names and the way they fight. A mountain tribe everyone assumes died out a generation ago. That is as much family as they have to give, and it accounts for the rest of it."

The fact went in behind Lord Aldric's eyes and was weighed. Cael looked at them with the first real interest he'd shown.

"You have rendered service to this house," Lord Aldric said at last. "Name what reward is appropriate."

Jianxin looked at Tianyi. Tianyi looked at the room. "Somewhere to sleep," Jianxin said.

Lord Aldric's mouth opened slightly and closed. Aldren blinked. Cael's expression went briefly still. The girl by the window looked down at her hands for the first time since they'd entered.

"Guest quarters," Lord Aldric said. "There's a training courtyard attached. If that suits."

"It suits."

---

Maren walked them out herself, and stopped in the corridor past her father's door.

"You came because I made you. That's discharged." The assessment was running again, the one that hadn't stopped since the treeline. "But the road stays closed a while yet, and you've nowhere to be, and my family has use for people who can do what you did on it. Escort work, mostly. Disputes among the vassals. The occasional harder thing. The pay is fair, the bed is real, and there's a physician on the staff." A beat. "Stay or don't. I'd rather you stayed."

Jianxin thought about four days of forest with no one to read, and a whole city behind these walls he hadn't touched, and the wall in his own gift he'd been beating against since the road, and the woman in front of him who was still the most interesting thing he'd found in any of it.

"What's the occasional harder thing?"

"The kind that involves people like the ones on the road." A pause. "And occasionally worse."

He looked at Tianyi, who had already run the numbers, and the numbers came out a roof, a physician, and a season to learn the shape of this place before they had to walk into it with nothing. Two drifters taking paid work in the densest concentration of readable people Jianxin had ever stood in, nothing owed and nothing sworn.

"We'll stay," Jianxin said. "For now."

The guest quarters were two rooms with a shared sitting area and a door onto a small courtyard. Jianxin stood at the window, looked at the courtyard, then lay back on the bed and thought about thirty-two intents and the thing that had run between six of them that he hadn't been able to touch. Somewhere in this city, behind walls like these, were people whose whole craft was the between. He meant to learn it from them, whether they meant to teach him or not.
