# Chapter 12

The comment hung in the air, and the room waited to see what would be done with it. The attention landed on Jianxin, the help who'd gotten familiar, and the room wanted to see whether the stray understood he'd been put in his place.

"You're right," Jianxin said pleasantly. "I don't have a house, or a name anyone here would know. I came off a road. You've placed me exactly." He let that sit. "It's good to be seen clearly. Most people get it wrong."

The man had expected a flush, or a stammer. He hadn't expected agreement, and agreement left him holding a raised hand with nothing to bring it down on. "A man should know his station," he said, recovering.

"He should. You clearly do." Jianxin smiled. "It must be a great comfort, having yours handed to you. Saves all the trouble of earning one."

A few heads turned. The man's color changed. "I traveled here in the service of a house four generations your better, to assess whether the match is suitable. I would mind your tongue."

"Of course. You're the one they send to do the looking." Jianxin nodded, warm, as though conceding a fine point. "Every house needs a good pair of eyes. It's honest work, looking, for a man not suited to the other kinds."

The man took a step closer, the pleasantness drained out of him, and what was under it was uglier and more honest. Jianxin watched it surface and felt the read settle into place. A small man given a small power by proximity to a large one, sent to a declining house so that for one evening he could be the one doing the measuring. The whole of him organized around the rare pleasure of looking down.

Jianxin turned his head, unhurried, and found Edric across the floor. "Lord Edric," he called, polite, carrying. "Your man's gotten off his lead. You might want to call him back before he embarrasses your house in someone else's hall."

It was a clean stroke and it landed clean, and the worst of it was that it asked Edric to do something, so everyone now watched to see whether he would. Edric looked over with the mild interest of a man watching weather through a window, did not move to intervene, and looked away again, and the not-answering was so complete that a few people shifted, uncomfortable, having watched a man decline to defend his own standing without seeming to know it was his.

The Edrath man saw it too. Saw that no one was coming. And something in him, denied the backing he'd assumed was behind him, abandoned the last of the propriety and reached for the one thing that would wound. He looked at where Lira had been standing.

"And you," he said. "A grown man, no house, no name, sweating over a girl of twelve on a dance floor. One does wonder what a stray really wants, getting his hands on the young daughter of a house he's attached himself to. Whether the dancing is all he's—"

Jianxin's hand was already moving.

It came up empty, the arm swinging through a draw that had no sword in it, the old motion his body made before his mind had finished deciding anything. And into the moving hand, between one instant and the next, a blade arrived. He did not see Tianyi do it. No one did. There was simply no sword, and then there was a sword, materialized into the grip mid-swing as though it had been waiting in the air for the hand.

The edge stopped against the man's throat, a hair from the skin, not a tremor in it.

The room had gone entirely silent.

The man's eyes dropped to the thing at his neck, and what he saw made no sense. It was not a sword, not really. It was stone, raw grey stone in the rough shape of a blade, and only along the cutting edge did it change, a thin seam of metal drawn out of the rock and laid down sharp, a hand's breadth of true edge on a length of mountain. The grip was unfinished, a column of the same stone never shaped for a hand because there had been no time to shape it. It had been made between one breath and the next, pulled whole out of the floor and the walls and the iron fittings of the hall and pressed into being in the half-second a man's arm took to swing. The edge against his throat, though, was perfect. Whatever else had been done in haste, the part that mattered had not.

He understood, dimly, that he was being held at the throat by something that had not existed a moment ago and had been built, in the time it took to want it, by someone in the room who was not even holding it.

---

The man's guard moved fast, a heavy figure shouldering through the frozen guests, hand going to his weapon, the air around him thickening as he pulled on whatever the Witness had given him. The pull found less than the man expected. The thickening came, and thinned, and came again unevenly, a god reached for at the far edge of its own ground, and the guard's jaw tightened at the shortfall even as he came on.

"Take the blade off him," the guard said, "or I'll put you down where you stand."

Jianxin didn't take the blade off him. He didn't look at the guard at all. He kept his eyes on the man whose throat he was holding, and read the guard without deciding to.

It was nothing. An empty suit. A career of standing behind someone important, of being large in rooms where largeness was enough, of a competence never once tested against anyone who could actually fight. The guard's intent came to Jianxin already finished, every move the man would make laid out before he made it, each habit and tell and trained reflex a single thread, and the threads ran together into one rope, and Jianxin had the whole of it in his hand before the guard had taken his next breath.

The guard drew and struck for Jianxin's free shoulder, a clean overhand meant to drop him, quick by the standard of men who guard doors. Jianxin had spent seven years being struck at by a brother who built his attacks to be unreadable, and against that standard the overhand arrived like a thing underwater. His free hand was already where the blade was going, not blocking, meeting it at the one point along its path where the flat could be turned, a touch, two fingers, and the cut slid wide and into the air. The guard corrected for the ribs, and the correction had been in the rope too, and Jianxin's fingers were waiting at the new line before the edge arrived, guiding it past, the whole of it looking less like defense than like a man helping a clumsy partner through a dance he hadn't learned.

The guard put his weight behind it and drove straight for the center of him, the simple killing thrust, no finesse left. Jianxin caught the blade a hand's width from his chest. Not cleanly. The edge bit into the two fingers that closed on it, and blood ran down the flat, and he held it anyway, because his fingers were exactly where the point was going to be, and a blade with nowhere to go does not go anywhere.

The guard wrenched and couldn't free it, the steel moving as though it had agreed to belong to someone else, and something in his face came apart. He reached for the last thing he had and began to pour his power into the blade, the Witness's gift, the air screaming thin around the steel.

"Don't," Jianxin said.

He said it as you'd tell a child not to touch a stove, and the guard, who an instant ago had been a large dangerous man in a room of his betters, stopped, because the boy holding his sword between two fingers had not raised his voice and had not looked at him and had not, in any moment of the exchange, appeared to be in any difficulty at all, and the animal under the cultivation understood before the man did that whatever this was, the power he was about to spend would buy him nothing but a clearer view of how far out of his depth he stood.

The thinning air subsided. The guard let go. The sword hung in Jianxin's two fingers, the blood running slow along the steel, and then he let it fall, and it rang on the floor in a silent hall.

"That's better," Jianxin said, to the man at his swordpoint, as though the guard had never spoken.

---

A bead of blood welled at the man's throat where the edge rested, swelled, and ran a thin line to his collar. He had not felt the blade move. It hadn't. He had swallowed.

"Enough."

Lord Aldric's voice came across the hall, and Jianxin took the blade away. The stone came apart in his hand, edge and haft losing their shape all at once and running back to grit that scattered across the floor at his feet, and he stood with his arms loose and his face open as though nothing had happened, the two cut fingers dripping a slow line into the grit.

Lord Aldric crossed the floor, looked at the Edrath man, at Jianxin, at the guard with his hand still on a weapon he hadn't drawn, and the calculation moved behind his face in real time. He could not punish the boy. Everyone had heard what the man was reaching for when the blade arrived, and there was no version of the Arbiter's necessity in which a grown man saying that about a child of the house could be the injured party.

"You will not raise steel in my hall again," Lord Aldric said to Jianxin, because something had to be said and that was the thing that cost him nothing.

"I won't need to," Jianxin said.

Casver stepped into the space the reprimand opened, his voice pitched low and grave and carrying just far enough. "Brother, this is precisely the difficulty." He did not look at Jianxin. He looked at the vassals still close enough to hear, and grieved, which was a more useful thing to do than argue. "We take in strays off the road, and the strays draw steel on the honored guests of the house we are trying to join ourselves to. I say nothing against the boy's courage. I say only that the region is watching how we keep our hall, and tonight they have seen us fail to keep it." He let it settle, sorrowful, unhurried. "A house that cannot govern its own people can hardly ask another house to—"

Maren looked at him.

Jianxin happened to be watching her face when she did it, and for one instant the thing he saw there stopped him cold. It was not anger. Anger he could have read. It was the same thing he had felt in the family court, the morning the blood fell on the brass and the scale tipped to the side of the law. The Arbiter. The god this house had given itself to, that this whole city knelt to, the one who weighs and judges and whose verdict ends the matter. He had felt it move through the room then, in the ritual, at a distance. Now it was looking out through a woman's eyes at her own uncle, and in them, for the space of a breath, were the scales themselves, level and patient and already settled, surfacing through her rather than done by her. For that instant the thing looking at Casver was the god her family served, and it had measured him completely and found the matter closed.

Casver closed his mouth, and would not finish the thought.

The weight passed. Maren turned back to her father. "This event is concluded," Lord Aldric said. "My family thanks its guests."

---

The man at the center of it had gone gray, and then the fear curdled into something else, because he had remembered what he stood behind.

"You can't touch me," he said, before the room could disperse, his voice climbing. "Any of you. I am of House Edrath. Elder Sevran's shelter is over me, and there is not a house in this region that would let one of its strays put a hand on a man under an Apostle's protection." He was looking at Lord Aldric now. "Account for your servant, or the match dies tonight, and I will tell my house precisely what I was made to stand through in yours."

The threat worked as threats worked in this room. It was true, and everyone knew it, and the truth of it settled over the hall and made the blade that had just been at his throat retroactively meaningless, a gesture that could never have been completed. Lord Aldric's jaw set, but he said nothing, because there was nothing the law would let him say.

The guests gathered themselves. The evening folded up around the wound in it. The brothers were sent to their rooms, framed as discipline, understood by everyone to be otherwise. The Edrath man's own delegation took him in hand before the Vaynes had to, and walked him out early and quietly, both houses letting it be seen that he was being removed, and that was the punishment. A slap on the wrist dressed as decorum. The alliance was worth more than his dignity, and so his dignity was what they spent.

Lira watched him go. She had been kept to the side through all of it, a hand on her shoulder, but she had seen the whole thing, the blade that came from nowhere and stopped exactly where it meant to, the man who'd made her step behind her sister walking out with his head down. And she had heard, before any of it, the thing Jianxin said on the floor, the promise about the blade and who it belonged to, and now she had watched it be true.

She looked at Jianxin as he was led out, and he caught the look, and there was something in it that had not been there before the evening. The helplessness was still in her. But it had something beside it now. She had seen that the walls could be cut, that someone would swing, that the swinging was real and not a story he'd told her to be kind.

---

"How hard would it be," Jianxin said, "for the two of us to kill an Apostle."

They were in their room, the lamp low. Jianxin had his own sword out and was moving through forms in the cleared space between the beds, slow, working the evening out of his arms one cut at a time, the two fingers of his left hand bound in linen Tianyi had produced and applied without being asked. Tianyi sat properly on the bed, because Tianyi sat properly.

"That's the question you came away with."

"He said we couldn't touch him. He was right, and it annoyed me, and now I want to know how right."

Tianyi set the question down a moment. "You felt what their guard pulled up. When he reached for his power."

"A little. It went nowhere."

"That's a Devoted. The lowest of the three grades these people have. They give themselves to a god, all the way, and the god gives something back, a steadiness, a competence, but only on their own god's ground. Off it, it thins to almost nothing. That man was on Arbiter soil with his Witness running thin, which is half of why he folded." He turned the lamp down a fraction. "Set against my own road, a Devoted sits about where I am now. Qi Gathering, the nine levels you start with before anything has a grander name. I'm four levels into it. A strong one, late into Qi Gathering? Together, if it went well, we could kill him. Alone, either of us loses. That's the floor. That's where we actually stand tonight, whatever it looked like in the hall."

"And the next one up."

"The Herald. Rare. The god has marked them specifically, answered them, given one real power that travels with them anywhere. A Herald can sit as an elder, hold land, advise a lord. The Vaynes fielded eight or nine at their height and called themselves a great house for it. Foundation Establishment, the stage past the nine levels, one whole step above me. Even a weak one, fresh into it, beats the two of us together. We'd land a few real hits. We might make it cost something. We would still lose, and we would not get to choose whether we walked away."

"And the old man's past that."

"A long way past. The Apostle is chosen directly. Not marked. Chosen. Given a power that bends the world around it, and there may be five of them alive in the whole world. An Apostle stands where a Golden Core stands, two full steps above me, and against a Golden Core the two of us don't last a single exchange. One blow, and there is no second one, and running isn't on the table, because you don't outrun a thing at that level." He looked at his brother. "If we met the old man as we are tonight, we'd be dead before either of us understood anything had begun. That's why the threat worked. It wasn't a bluff. There is genuinely nothing the two of us could do to a house with an Apostle in it. That's what the marriage is buying. A thing that cannot be touched."

"And paying with her."

Tianyi didn't answer that.

Jianxin stopped moving and stood with the sword loose at his side, and thought about the Golden Core, the power a god reached down and placed in a man, the strength that held up a whole house without the house ever having to lift anything. And under that he thought about the thing Tianyi had said in passing and not dwelt on, that the gods gave the power, that all of it ran back to them, the Apostle and the Herald and the Devoted and the steadiness in the soil of every territory he'd crossed, all of it flowing out from somewhere.

"One day," he said, "I'd like to be able to touch a thing like that."

"One day," Tianyi said, "you might." He was quiet a moment. "There will be consequences in the morning. Not from the Vaynes, they can't move against you without admitting the insult warranted it. But Casver will find a use for it, and Maren will spend the morning containing whatever he does."

"I know."

"You don't regret it."

"No."

"Neither do I." Tianyi set the records aside and lay down, and after a while his breathing went even, and the room was quiet.

Jianxin stayed up a while with the sword. He moved through one more form, slow, the last of the evening finally gone out of his arms, and at the end of it he let the blade come to rest where it wanted to rest. It did not point at the wall, or the door, or anything in the room. It drifted off true as it settled and came to bear past all of it, the point lifting slightly, following the slope of the land itself, inward and up, toward the one direction the room did not contain.

He didn't know what was up there. He only knew the sword had chosen it without him, steady, repeatable, returning to the same bearing each time he let it go. Somewhere along that line was the thing all of this came from, the grades and the soil and the old man's untouchable strength and the marriage and the girl paying for it, all of it fed from a single source he could feel the direction of and could not yet see.

The blade held, very still, pointed at the gods.
