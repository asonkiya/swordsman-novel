# Chapter 1

The Void. A place where light refused to linger and sound died before it was born. No air, no ground, no horizon, only an endless stretch of nothing that pressed close and cold. Even thought scattered here, as if the Dao had turned its gaze away.

Yet in the abyss there lay a woman. Her body was withered past recognition and still it carried a majesty that would have put mortals on their knees. At a closer look her belly was swollen, and from it a divine aura made itself known, etching laws into the dark, manifesting something past names or words. It was supreme. It was one. It was Dao.

The light of it fell on two fetuses. One radiated a clean and flawless aura, refined, beautiful, and beside its neighbor it was a firefly beside the sun. The other gave off nothing. It did not bask in the light. It released it. Unmoving, unshaped, absolute, the origin from which all the rest seemed to flow.

The Void trembled. No ripple, no warning, only sudden motion as a fissure split the dark and a man stepped through. He moved with calm finality, his presence throwing long shadows even here where light held no power. Behind him came a mortal doctor, his steps unhurried, his eyes holding only clarity. This was a man whose command over flesh and qi had long since passed beyond reverence.

They came to the woman. Her cultivation had been sealed, her breath buried beneath silence, and even so her presence lingered like a fading order. The doctor knelt, unrolled instruments clean and gleaming, and set a hand on her belly. Light moved beneath his fingers.

"It worked," he said.

The man nodded, unable to hide the joy that softened his gaze. "Begin."

The scalpel moved with absolute certainty. A small incision opened and light spilled out in steady waves. The doctor reached in and drew forth the first child. Its aura was sound, a clean and ordinary completeness, the kind that in some quiet mortal household would have marked a boy as gifted and been the proud talk of a village for a generation. A good root. A fine son. Nothing the heavens would trouble themselves to notice. Set beside the radiance still waiting in the womb, it was simply small.

The doctor reached again. The second child emerged, silent, without glow, and the Void pulled back from it. This one held no pulse and no sign of life by any mortal measure. Yet its presence was undeniable. This was not a child born of Dao. This was Dao, given form.

In silence the doctor wrapped the newborns, cleaned his tools, and stepped away. "It's done."

The man received the children.

Then the emperor drew forth a strand of qi, and another light filled the Void, shaped where the first had been wild, its every pulse carrying one clear intent. A key. Under its glow, lines appeared across the woman's withered form, patterns that defied language, each one pure intent, and without sound the qi sank into the heart of the formation.

Moments passed. Then the woman drew breath.

It came slow and certain. Her limbs uncurled, her skin returned to fullness, the seals on her cultivation fell away like frost under sun. She stood. Her expression was calm, but her aura was scattered, her movements uneven, as if a seasoned fighter had been handed back a body she no longer remembered.

Her voice came quiet and steady, and even in weakness her thoughts were not of herself.

"Were there any traces of my Dao left in him?"

The man shook his head. "None. His innate physique is untouched. Your suppression was complete."

She closed her eyes and exhaled. "Good. Even a trace would have undone everything."

He looked down at the child in his arms, still and silent and absolute. "There was no interference. He is what he was always meant to be."

She nodded. "Then it begins."

She stepped forward and he placed the children in her arms. One slept, his breath warm and even. The other lay still, the air around him shifting, resisting contact. She held them both and lifted her eyes to the Void, and her gaze pierced its layers until it settled on a single realm.

By the standards of realms it was small. Its shape, though, was strange. The whole world sloped upward toward its center, a colossal mountain rising from its edges. It circled no sun, and no stars hung in its sky. Even so, time moved, seasons turned, life went on. The rhythm of the place followed a single object, a rock, resting now halfway up the colossal slope.

The emperor watched her and saw the weight in her eyes. He set a gentle hand on her shoulder.

"You know this is the only way. The Innate Dao Embryo can't survive under the full weight of the Heavenly Dao."

"I know that, Ke'er." She did not look away from the realm. "What I don't know is whether the place will keep. The Dragonkin is close to making the leap. And the madman south of the slope is closer than the clan wants to admit to whatever it is he means to do." Her arms tightened on the children. "We are setting them down in the one thin-Dao world in a prosperous age, and the very thing that keeps it thin is the thing most likely to kill them."

He sighed. "A world like this is hard to find. We won't find another."

"No." She watched the rock on the slope a while. "Has the clan at least made certain that lunatic doesn't pull the whole of it into the fire he's building?"

He did not answer at once, and she did not ask again.

---

Years later, in a small garden, two children sparred, and it might have been a heartwarming thing, two brothers at play, had one of them not moved like something the world had broken its own rules to make.

The twins shared a face and nothing else. On one side stood a boy surrounded by his own power; flame, water, and wind turned around him with flawless precision, as though the heavens had bent down to set each mote on its course. Every blade of grass and flicker of fire became a weapon in his hands. A spark would catch the eye an instant before a thread of wind drove for the heart. In his black eyes the world unfolded as a board, heaven and earth reduced to pieces in play. His name matched his nature. Tianyi.

His opponent did not move with the same ease. The second boy fought like fire catching in green wood, all spirit and effort, throwing everything he had into a blade that did not yet know what it wanted to be. There was something in it. Anyone could have seen that. A sword reaching for shapes beyond its years, a fisherman's patience one moment and a duelist's lunge the next, fragments of things he'd read or been shown, snatched at and half-formed. But they came and went without order. The seams showed. For every shape that landed, two came apart in his hands. He was not channeling the swordsmen of ages past. He was a boy imitating them, badly, joyfully, giving ground the whole while and grinning as though he hadn't noticed, or didn't care that he had. His blade was still only learning how to say his name. Jianxin.

Their swords clashed and rang like chimes beneath the morning sun. Ripples ran across the pond and scattered the reflections on the garden walls.

Jianxin broke away first, sliding back over the grass, his grin flashing. "If you'd moved just then," he said, pointing his sword, "it would've been over. You had the opening. You think too much."

Tianyi exhaled softly, his gaze unshaken. The air around him stirred, faint motes of light following the rhythm of his breath. "And yet here you stand, out of stance, breathing unevenly. Perhaps it's better I waited."

Jianxin clicked his tongue and feinted a strike. "You always hide behind that calm. If the heavens fell I swear you'd still be counting clouds."

"Counting keeps one from being crushed beneath them."

"Maybe. It also keeps you from living." Jianxin lunged, laughing under his breath.

Their exchange became a blur, Jianxin's blade darting with unrestrained want, Tianyi's movements smooth and inevitable, his power flowing around him as a river follows its bed. And the harder Jianxin pressed, the heavier the air seemed to grow. Tianyi never struck first, never forced an opening, and still the world tilted in his favor. Fire bent to his stance. Wind shifted to meet his need. The ground answered his feet like an old companion.

It's like fighting underwater, Jianxin thought, his jaw tightening. Every step, the current pushes me back.

He grit his teeth and surged forward anyway, his blade coming down in a fierce arc at Tianyi's shoulder, the best strike he had, his whole small weight behind it.

Tianyi did not trouble to break it. The wind caught the line of the cut and set it somewhere else, like a hand turning aside a reaching child, and Jianxin's own momentum carried him past, arm overextended, chest open. Before he could recover, Tianyi's palm struck gently against his shoulder. A pulse of force rippled out and sent him skidding back across the garden stones. He stopped at the pond's edge, the surface trembling from the displaced air.

There was only the rustle of leaves, the drip of water.

By any measure the world below kept, the boy who lost was a wonder. The trouble was that he had been set down, from the first breath he drew, beside the one measure the world had no name for. He looked at his trembling sword hand, and his breathing steadied though his heart did not, and his mind was already turning over why the strike had failed.

He picked himself up off the stones, still grinning, and did not appear to mind.

"One day," he said under his breath, lowering the blade, "I'll drag you into the water with me."

Tianyi gave no reply. But for an instant a faint smile touched his lips, and the two brothers made their way back inside, shoulder to shoulder, as they had always been.
