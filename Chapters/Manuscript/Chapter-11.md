# Chapter 11

The tailors came on the sixth day, four of them, with two attendants and a look from Maren that did not invite discussion.

They had an easy time of Tianyi, who gave them nothing to correct, every part of him brought up together, the body of someone whose training had been complete rather than aimed. They had a harder time of Jianxin. Their eyes went to his shoulders, his arms, the line of his back, never his face. He was built as the sword wanted him built, the shoulder and forearm and the long muscle down the sword arm exactly what one kind of work makes, the rest of him lean and in service to it, the whole of him oriented around a point just past his hip where nothing was. Complete in itself, and completed around an absence.

They had laid out something with a great many pieces for him. He turned it over, put it down, and took a simpler thing off the rack, one dark layer, a single line of deep amber at the collar.

"That isn't what we prepared," the head tailor said.

"I know. This one."

The tailor looked at it, then at him, and started the adjustments without arguing.

When they finished it was the same face on both brothers and nothing else the same. Tianyi wore his still and settled, a composure he had never once had to put on. Jianxin wore the same face like it might do anything next.

"You look like you're about to rob someone," Tianyi said.

"I look excellent."

"Both can be true."

---

They were announced at the top of the stairs, and Jianxin felt the room change as they came down, a small lull, conversations losing their thread, eyes finding them and staying. Nobody here knew how to place them, so the room simply looked. The young women looked at Jianxin. The older guests looked at Tianyi, taking their time, reading how he stood, how he held the room without seeming to want it.

Tianyi found a place near the east wall where he could see all of it. Jianxin walked toward the nearest knot of guests, and it was easier than it had any right to be.

He was only interested in them, each one a different thing to work out, and the interest did the rest. Everyone in the room had put something on at the door, a role, a borrowed standing, a careful face, and he found it more fun to talk to the person holding it up than to the thing being held, until somewhere in the talking they forgot they were holding anything.

A young man from a provincial house had been stiff all night, every sentence weighed before he let it go, certain one wrong word would give him away as not belonging. Jianxin leaned in like a fellow criminal. "Tell me the worst thing about a party like this. I've never been to one. I won't know if you're exaggerating." The young man reached for the polite answer, caught the grin, and gave the real one instead, and by the third exchange he was laughing too hard to remember to guard his sentences.

There was a woman his own age near the windows with her whole situation written in the things she stepped around: an engagement she hadn't chosen, a man she hadn't met, an evening spent being gracious about both. Jianxin went nowhere near any of it. He'd caught her looking too long at the open doors to the garden, so when he reached her he tipped his head toward them. "You keep looking out there. Is it better?"

"Everything's better than in here," she said, before she could stop herself, and then looked faintly alarmed at having said it aloud.

He grinned like she'd won something. She told him, helpless not to, about a tree she used to climb before she was told ladies didn't, and for a minute she was laughing at herself, present, not the thing being married off but a girl who'd skinned both knees getting down, and when he let the talk go she watched him cross the room and wanted the minute back.

An older merchant with no real standing had tucked himself against a wall to make the corner look like a choice. Jianxin went and stood in it with him as though it were the best seat in the house, and within minutes three other guests had drifted over to see what they were missing, and the man was holding court in the very spot he'd picked to disappear in, and none of them could have said how.

The whole time, under it, he was reaching for the between. Two guests who'd come together passed a decision across a half-second of eye contact, and he reached for what ran in the gap, and came away empty. The wall was still there. He kept working the room and kept failing at the one thing he'd come to this city to learn, and found he didn't mind the failing as long as there were this many people to fail against.

Lira was by the musicians, watching the two of them since they'd come down the stairs. When Jianxin caught her at it she looked away fast, then back, the looking-away a beat too late to have worked. She was terrible at it. He almost said something, remembered she was twelve, and let her keep watching.

---

The family came in together, the room turning toward Lord Aldric on its own. Lady Isara was at his side, her smile arriving on time and sized exactly to the occasion, her attention spread evenly across the room with nothing left to accident. Jianxin read her in passing and found the gap she lived in. She knew everything the dinner was meant to paper over, the decline, that the match would not fix it, all of it sitting behind her eyes, fully seen and entirely unsayable, and the only place her knowing was allowed to go was into the precision of the smile. She managed the one thing she was permitted to manage, and managed it perfectly, and it was the smallest cage Jianxin had read in the house. Cael came half a step out of the evening's rhythm, in the room without quite belonging to it. He'd made his position clear and been overruled, and now he was required to stand in a room celebrating the thing he'd argued against. Maren came last, and by the time she entered the evening was already running on something she'd set in motion hours before.

Jianxin read her in the doorway. The composure was complete, and under it, faint, a woman watching a thing she'd built to see whether it would hold. It was holding. She wasn't letting herself feel that yet.

He drifted toward the east wall, where Tianyi had found Maren, or Maren had found Tianyi, both having wanted the same spot for the same reason. He came in at the edge of it and stayed there, half-listening, his attention mostly on the floor.

"Drevant will hold tonight," Tianyi was saying. "He can't move on you with Venne in the room. You sat them together on purpose."

She didn't say yes.

"He's been backing away a month," she said. "I can't find why."

"The northern route." Tianyi's eyes were on the room. "His land sits on it. It's been dying for years. He can't say so without saying the house is weaker than it was, so he says other things. The road dispute. The points of order. The same complaint in different clothes."

Jianxin caught the words at the edge of his attention, the northern route, the dying land, and they slid past him, because across the floor the provincial young man had just been cut by someone's wife and was deciding whether to pretend he hadn't been, and that was a live thing, a person in motion, and the route was only a line on a map. He filed *northern route* somewhere shallow and went back to the floor. He had Drevant. Whatever was on a map didn't change what was in him.

"It isn't only him," Tianyi went on. "Half the houses here are taxed on roads that emptied a decade ago. The ones who quietly moved their trade are fine. The ones who didn't are going under and calling it luck."

"That isn't in the records."

"It's in the convoy logs and the guild filings. You have to read them next to the tax rolls, and nobody does, because the men who keep one don't talk to the men who keep the other." A pause. "And under that, the numbers coming up from the districts don't hold together. Not by much. They all lean the same way. The administrators aren't lying. They're surviving. A district whose numbers look bad loses something, so the numbers never look bad. Everyone shades a little, all in the same direction, and the books still balance, so no one calls it dishonest."

Maren was watching him now with an attention she hadn't given anything else all night. "And the people who decide things decide off numbers that were bent before they got there."

"Bent by people who believe in the thing they're bending. That's what makes it hold. A man who doubted would check his figures twice. They don't doubt."

She let out a breath that was almost a laugh and had nothing funny in it. "Two years I've been in those records. Sure I was missing the big one. There was no big one. Just the small ones. All leaning."

"You weren't missing anything."

Across the floor, the glances had begun to accumulate, and not on the family. On Jianxin. The boy with no name anyone could place, no house, no standing, who'd worked half the room and was now standing at the family's shoulder as though he belonged there. The glances weren't hostile. They were the glances of a room running its accounting, locating what didn't fit.

---

Lira made her careful way toward him through the thinning crowd, ears red, jaw set, braced for him to laugh at her.

He didn't laugh. It was a near thing, and it was fond.

"Do you want to dance?" she said, fast, before she could lose it.

"Yes." He held out his hand.

The first steps were wrong and he knew it the instant he took them, his weight wanting to fall where a sword wanted it and not where a partner did. He moved through it anyway and felt exactly where it failed. Then he looked at the floor. The other dancers turned past him and he read them, the shift of weight before the turn, the half-beat where one partner waited on the other, the small pressure of a hand that told a body where to go. It came at him fast and bright, the rise and fall, the give and take, all of it learned and folded in between one step and the next. By the second pass he wasn't fumbling. By the third he was leading.

And the whole time he was reading her, because he could not not, and she read like nothing else in the room. A child mid-becoming, shifting under his attention, reaching a hundred directions at once, no version of her finished, the floor and the ceiling of her nowhere near each other. He had read exactly this once before, on a road, in fourteen men, in the breath before he closed every direction each of them had been reaching in. The reading was the same. Only what he did with it had been different. He turned Lira through the figure and felt the fixed dead flicker somewhere under the living girl in his hands, and held them both, because that was the price of the gift and he was not going to look away from it, and he kept his face easy so the child would not see what had crossed it.

"You couldn't do that a minute ago," Lira said.

"I can now. I watched."

She stared like he'd done a card trick she couldn't catch the seam of, then laughed, surprised out of her nervousness.

They turned through the next figure. He could feel her working up to something, the questions stacking behind the small ones.

"Where did you grow up?"

"A long way from here. In the mountains. There was a school, of a sort. Just me and my brother and the people who taught us."

"Only the two of you?"

"Only the two of us." A turn. "Nobody told us what we were supposed to want."

Her eyes came up at that, quick. "Nobody told you what you were supposed to want."

"No."

She considered it, slow and serious. "Everyone tells me what I'm supposed to want. All the time. They don't say it like that. They say I'll understand when I'm older." A pause. "Maren says it now too. She didn't used to. She used to argue with Father, about things she thought were unfair. Now she just finds the cleverest way to do what he wants and everyone says how much she's grown." The frown deepened. "It doesn't look like growing to me."

"What does it look like."

"Like she went quiet somewhere inside and never came back out." She said it simply, and it was the most exact reading of Maren he'd heard from anyone in the house. Then, not dramatic, just asking: "Is it going to happen to me?"

He looked at the clear eyes, the open face, a person this house hadn't finished with yet, and turned her through the figure while he decided how honest to be. He decided all the way.

"It'll happen to you if you let it." He turned her through the figure, light, as though they were only talking about the music. "It's happening to her because she's decided there's no other door. That every road's already been walked for her, and all that's left is to walk it well." He held her eyes. "You don't have to decide that."

"What if there isn't another door."

"Then make one."

She blinked at him.

"If you look and there's no door, you tear one open yourself. In the wall. Wherever you want it." A turn through the figure. "That's all a door is. A hole someone decided to make."

She looked around the room without meaning to, the dinner, the ceremony, the house that had stood for generations and would stand long after everyone in it was dust. "These walls look pretty solid."

"They always do." He smiled. "Right up until someone puts a blade through one."

"I don't have a blade."

He stopped. Not the dance. The lightness, the part of him that had been keeping all of this a game for her. He let the music carry them a turn while he looked at her, and when he spoke again there was nothing easy left in it.

"You will. Everyone does. Yours won't look like mine, and it doesn't have to."

"Maren can use a sword." There was an edge under it, the first he'd heard from her. "She's had one since before I was born. It hasn't cut her a door."

"Because it isn't hers. She set her own down somewhere and picked up the one this house handed her, and a borrowed blade cuts what its owner wants cut." The music carried them a few steps. "I'm not talking about steel. There will come a day you stand in front of a wall that everyone you love has told you is the whole world, and on the far side of it is the thing you actually want, the one nobody ever gave you the words for." He turned her slow and did not let her look away. "On that day you do not wait to be told it's allowed. You do not look around to see who's watching. You do not ask."

"What do I do."

"You pick up your sword."

He said it like the plainest fact in the world, and the truest one he had.

"And you swing first. Before the fear, before the second thought, before a single soul can talk you out of it. That part is yours alone. No one living gets to do it for you, or take it from you." The music turned and he turned her with it, and let the last of it come down gentle. "But the moment after you've swung, you won't be alone in the cut. That much I can promise you."

She held his eyes a long moment, twelve years old, something settling into her, quiet and sure.

---

By the third pass the glances had sharpened, and the comment came from near the door, not whispered, said at the volume of something meant to carry.

"One does wonder what Lady Maren is thinking," the man said, "letting the help get familiar with the young lady of the house at a formal occasion." He'd come with the Edrath delegation, minor family or senior attendant, his function to represent the standards of the house he traveled with. "If the Vayne household runs this loosely, Lord Edrath's family may want to reconsider whether the match suits their standing."

The room heard it. These rooms always heard it. Lira heard it. Her hands tightened on his once, fast, and then she stepped behind him, the body finding cover before the mind had caught up.

He stood still and let her. Across the floor, Maren had already started toward them, not fast, at the pace of a woman with somewhere to be that happened to be here.

And at the wall, Tianyi had gone quiet mid-sentence, his eyes moving from the Edrath man to the empty space where the evening's good order had been a moment ago, already counting what it would cost.
