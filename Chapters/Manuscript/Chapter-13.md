# Chapter 13

Maren was at her desk when they came in, correspondence drifted across it, and she didn't look up from the letter she was reading.

"The Edrath delegation sent word. They'll proceed with the match, so that much of last night is behind me." Her mouth tightened. "The rest isn't. Drevant withdrew his support this morning. For good, this time. Not a point of order. A withdrawal."

"He'll come back," Jianxin said. "I told you. I read him. He's honest, he isn't your enemy, and once the killings stop he'll—"

"He withdrew, Jianxin." She set the letter down. "I know he's honest. I've known Drevant since I was small. His honesty is the problem, not the cure for it." She pressed two fingers to the bridge of her nose. "Go and see him. He won't grant me an audience today, but he'll receive you, which is more than he'll do for me. Sit in his front room, let him pour you tea, and tell me what's underneath it." A beat. "And don't tell him I sent you."

Jianxin went, and did not trouble to wonder what he would find.

---

The carriage took them across the city faster than he'd have liked, the noble tier ordered and spotless, then the streets roughening and narrowing as the ground fell toward the river, all of it gone before he'd had a proper look. Another day, he decided. He'd walk it on his own feet then.

Lord Drevant received them in the front room, not the study. The room a house keeps for people it does not mean to know better. He was a small, neat man gone soft and white at the edges, and he was pleased to see them, or did a fond imitation of it.

"The swordsman." He said it to Jianxin, pouring before anyone had asked. "I heard what happened in the hall. Sit, both of you." He raised his voice toward the back of the house. "Mira. The cold pork, the plum cakes, whatever bread's left. These two look starved to me."

Jianxin was already sitting. "I've never had a plum cake."

"You'll have an opinion shortly."

When the tray came he had a cake off it before it touched the table and made a sound around it he didn't trouble to muffle. Drevant watched him eat with plain delight, his table too long without a real appetite at it, and talked, the price of grain, the bad seed-year, a son posted south, and Jianxin let him talk and read him the whole while and found exactly what he'd told Maren he would find. Honest. No false floor. A fondness in him that cost him nothing to give and that he gave anyway. The certainty settled in him warm as the tea. He had the man, the same as he'd had him the first day.

"You'd not credit it now," Drevant said, settling back, "but I took my father's seat younger than you are. Reed-thin, and certain as iron." He turned the cup in his hands. "There's an old chapel at Hollowmere. I stood in it the day the seat came to me, and I swore to the Arbiter that I would do anything for the good of my land. Anything at all. Not to a house. Not to a name. To the ground itself and the souls standing on it." He smiled. "People hear an oath like that and think only of the soft duties in it. Guard them. Keep their peace. And it asks those, surely." The smile cooled. "It asks the hard ones too. To set down a thing you've carried for years, the morning it stops doing your people any good. To loose yourself from a house that has become—"

He stopped. The smile came back, worn and courteous. "You're her men, and it's my own tea I'd be saying it over. I'll leave it there. She has my respect. She has always had it."

There it is, Jianxin thought. He's leaving the Vaynes and it grieves him, and the grief is clean, and there's no malice anywhere in it. I had him from the first day.

He'd have staked anything on it.

"And this season," Drevant went on, quieter, "a man came into my villages."

The room changed. Not in Drevant. Around him.

"He goes in at night, into the houses of the devout, and kills them where they sleep, and I cannot lay a hand on him. Seven now. The last a deacon. Before him an old woman who'd done nothing in eighty years but keep her lamp lit for the Witness." His hands had gone still around nothing. "My watchmen are away. My strength is not the strength it was. A man came to my hall a fortnight past and told me his daughter had been taken in the night, and I sat in my own seat with nothing to give him. Not justice. Not the man's name. Nothing at all."

He was quiet a moment. "That is what a broken oath feels like, young man. Not the breaking of it. The sitting there afterward, both hands empty, in front of someone you swore to."

Then, into his cup: "And the dead stay, you know. The priests say they're weighed and passed on, and I've a chapel full of doctrine at Hollowmere that says so. I stood eleven years by my wife's grave, and I'll tell you what any widower on this world will tell you if you get enough tea in him. They don't go anywhere. They stay right where you set them down, and whatever it is that's meant to carry them off never comes." He said it mildly, a man remarking on the weather of his own life, and refilled the pot.

Jianxin had stopped chewing. A dead bird in a forest, four days out of the gate, the absence with an edge where a thing that should have gone had not finished going. He'd put it with the sky and the slope, and here it was again, poured out over tea by a man who thought he was saying something everyone knew. He'd been reaching the whole while, under the fondness and the pork and the chapel at Hollowmere, and where his reach had closed on nothing beneath the leaving of the Vaynes, here it found no bottom at all, the grief for the dead in those villages going down past where he could follow and not stopping. He let his food go cold in front of him.

And as he sat in that grief, the certainty he'd carried in warm turned over in his hands and showed him its other side.

He'd read the man right. Every word of what he'd told Maren was true. Drevant was honest, oath-bound, no enemy of hers, the realest thing in any room. And it changed nothing. The man was leaving precisely because he was all of those things. The honesty Jianxin had read so cleanly was the very thing taking Drevant out the door, because an honest man sworn to his land over any house could not sit in a court arguing roads while his people were knifed in their beds by a thing that house had left him no strength to fight. Jianxin had read the soul to the floor and been right about every inch of it, and the answer to the only question that mattered, would Drevant hold, had never been in the soul at all. It had been in the oath, and the land, and a dying road he hadn't bothered to look at, in the space between the man and the world that the man himself could not have shown him no matter how deep Jianxin reached.

He looked at Tianyi. Tianyi was looking at the room.

They thanked Lord Drevant, who pressed the last of the bread on them at the door and told them twice more to eat, and they went.

---

Outside, Jianxin walked a while without speaking. He'd eaten too much, and it sat in him beside the rest of it.

"I had him," he said finally. "Every word. Honest, oath-bound, no malice, no enemy of hers. I read him on the first day and I was right." He stopped on the street. "And it didn't matter. He's gone, and he's gone because of exactly the thing I read in him, and I told Maren he'd hold."

"You read the man," Tianyi said. "The man was what you read. Whether he'd hold was never only in the man."

"The northern route." Jianxin said it slowly. He could hear his brother's voice from the dinner, six days back, laying the whole of it on the table while he watched a stranger across the floor decide whether to pretend he hadn't been cut. *His land sits on it. It's been dying for years.* "You said it at the dinner. The route, the land. You'd asked for the figures before we ever sat down, and I half-heard all of it and put it down, because I had the man and I thought the man was the whole story."

"It's how you're built." Tianyi didn't make it a comfort and didn't make it a blow, only set it down. "Same as Casver. Same as the council. The thing that beats us in this city has never once been inside anyone, and you keep looking in the one place it isn't."

Jianxin stood with it. The plum cakes, the chapel at Hollowmere, the old woman and her lamp, and under all of it the flat fact that his gift had a floor, and the floor was the exact place the danger lived.

It did not make him want the gift less. It made him want the other thing. The between. Whatever it took to learn it.

"That old woman with her lamp," he said. "There's no bottom to it. He'll carry her till he dies." A pause. "And so will the man whose daughter got taken, and he hasn't even got a name to carry. Just the empty hands."

"No," Tianyi said. "He won't, if we change it."

Jianxin looked over at him.

"The road back to Drevant runs through the killings. Maren said it. Stop them, and his land is safer with this house than without it, and a man who goes where his oath sends him goes back to the house that made his people safe." Tianyi watched the street ahead, the dray traffic, the tiers climbing away above them. "You can't read the between yet. I can't put a blade in a thing the watch can't catch. Drevant's honest and it didn't save his villages, and Casver's careful and the law won't let anyone touch him, and none of that is a thing either of us can fix from this city by being right about people." He glanced at his brother. "But a man going into houses at night and killing the people in them is not a structure. It's a man. And a man can be found, and a man can be stopped, and that is the one corner of all of this that is actually ours to take."

Jianxin felt the last of the failure turn, in his chest, into something with a direction. "Then we go south."

"Then we go south."

---

Maren hadn't moved, though the stacks had. Jianxin gave her the short of it, and saying it took some of the noise out of him.

"You were right about him," he said. "And so was I. He's honest, and he's gone, and he's gone because he's honest. I read the man and missed the thing that moved him, and you don't need me to tell you which of those two facts matters." He turned a chair around and sat on it backward. "The road back to him is the killings. Stop them and he comes home. We're going to stop them."

She looked at him a moment, and something in her face eased that had nothing to do with the vassal court. "I know about the killings. Since spring. The men who'd hunt it are sitting on our border because I asked for them and he gave them." Her jaw set. "Casver's been sending him surveyors and clerks ever since, very generously, and we sent him the upkeep on a watch that isn't there. A house that takes a man's strength and leaves his people to die in their beds is not one his oath will let him keep. So he kept the oath, and he left us, and he was right to."

"He was right to," Jianxin agreed. It cost him less to say than it had on the street.

He turned the empty cup in his hand. "He sends his respect, by the way. Said you'd had it since you were small and couldn't let a thing lie unhandled." He let that land, then ruined it. "I may have added that you'd graduated to working everyone around you to the bone as well."

"You did not."

"I implied it warmly."

She picked the pen back up. "You're very relaxed, for a man with no name and no coin and a borrowed roof."

"That's the secret of it. Nothing to lose." He grinned. "You can smile, you know. Only Halric's here, and he's seen you worse."

She didn't smile. But she held his eyes a moment before she drew the next sheet from the pile.

"It's far country, and it isn't safe, and the proper authorities have already failed at it," Maren said. "Which means it's exactly the kind of thing you two are for." She was on her feet. "I'll come for it. Too much turns on the timing, and I won't run it from a desk on letters that take three days each way."

Halric had already set the correspondence aside and begun a list. "The closed carriage. Provisioned for four days, a change of horses at Harlow. The papers will say we're touring the grain tithes, and I've sent ahead for rooms that keep quiet." He didn't look up. "And the stomach draught. Every house up there will set oil and pastry in front of you, and I know how it sits with you."

"Thank you, Halric."

"Mm."

Jianxin stood and looked out the window, south and down, where the tiers fell away toward the rim and the land ran out past the city toward villages he'd never seen, full of people he'd never read, and one among them who went into houses at night and left empty hands behind him. He could not read the space between people. Not yet. But a man was a man, and the man was out there, and for the first time since the forest Jianxin knew exactly which way his own feet wanted to go.

"When do we leave," he said.

"Dawn," said Maren.

He was glad it wasn't sooner only because he wanted one walk through the lower city first, on his own feet, to read every face in it before he left. He'd start tonight. The carriage rolled at dawn, and the brothers would be on it, south, toward the one corner of all of it that was theirs to take.
