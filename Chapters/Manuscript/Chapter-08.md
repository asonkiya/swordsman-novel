# Chapter 8

The courtyard was empty when Jianxin came down at dawn. He sat against the eastern wall with the sword across his knees and didn't draw it yet, because he was thinking about the spirit root again.

It sat in him untouched, a heaven grade root the clan had confirmed before he could walk. The highest orthodox grade there was. People built whole lineages around roots two tiers beneath it. He'd been told what it meant before he was old enough to weigh it, and the choice of what to do with it had been waiting for him at the gate when he walked out of the institute.

The clan had made their position plain. Not through the senior instructor, who'd said almost nothing, but through the sealed package on Tianyi's desk, which had held among other things a letter to Jianxin laying out the situation without warmth or pressure, facts for someone old enough to be responsible for them. And a cultivation manual. A sword daoist path, tailored for a heaven grade root, orthodox and proven and mapped all the way up. He'd read it in two hours, understood it completely, and set it aside. It was a good manual. The trouble was that it made him a Daoist who used the sword, and that was the wrong order. The sword was the thing. Whatever else came would come through the sword or it wouldn't come at all.

He understood the argument against him. Cultivation built on what you brought to it, and an edge secured at the very bottom did not stay a single edge. It compounded through the realms until two cultivators who began a hair apart stood in different leagues by the heights. That was why the grade of a root mattered so much, not the size of the head start but that the advantage itself grew. And the orthodox road had been shaped over uncounted generations to run with the grain of the world, every realm a step the Dao itself seemed to have laid down in advance. It ran up through realms most cultivators never saw the top of. Where the brothers stood now, the nine small levels at the very bottom, was the first step of a mountain so tall that the people who'd mapped it had died on its lower slopes calling it endless.

A heaven grade root set on that road would have gone far. The clan knew it. They had told him what he was declining, not as a threat but as a fact he should carry knowingly.

And he could feel, now, exactly what he was setting down. Tianyi had started cultivating in the forest, and Tianyi was already pulling ahead, a density to him that hadn't been there a week ago, the orthodox path doing precisely what the clan said it did. For the first time since they were nine the gap between them was widening instead of closing, and it would keep widening as long as Jianxin sat in courtyards with a sword he'd built from storybooks while his brother climbed a ladder with no top. He had a ladder of his own offered to him, the same kind, near as good. He had only to pick it up and the gap would close.

He turned the manual over in his memory one more time, felt the whole golden length of the road it offered, and set it down again, his eyes open, knowing to the inch what it was worth. A road someone else had built and measured and laid in advance was the one thing he had spent sixteen years learning he did not want. Let Tianyi have the mountain with the steps cut into it. Jianxin would find out where his own feet went.

It cost him something, and he let it cost, and stood, and drew the sword, and got to work.

---

He started with the front-pusher.

He'd watched the man on the road and now he swung as the man had swung, the same overhand cuts, heavy and committed and a half-beat too eager. He ran the motion until his arm knew it, and it rang hollow. Not the form. The form was right. But the sword seemed to ask him why. Why this swing, why this weight.

He thought about the shoulder. The whole shoulder going into a stroke that needed only the forearm. The Vayne escorts had moved efficiently, every motion sized to its purpose. This had not been that. This was the swing of a man who'd spent years driving something heavier than a sword, who'd built his arms for a different tool and carried that building into a fight it was never shaped for. An axe, maybe, or a hoe, or the haft of a cart. Why was a laboring man on that road.

He swung again and let the question sit. Thirty-two men didn't appear on a road by accident. They'd been paid. So the man had been given coin to be there, and the blade told him where the coin had gone. Nicked along the edge, the grip rewrapped badly over old cord, a sword bought from someone who needed to sell it. A man who'd taken an advance for dangerous work and not spent it on the tool that might keep him alive. The eyes had been red at the edges before anything started, the commitment carrying a tremor under it, a body pushed past its natural state by something taken. So the coin had gone to pleasure, the kind that runs out and has to be bought again, and in the fight his whole body had pointed past the escorts at the carriage, at the money inside, the thing he kept spending faster than he could earn it. He hadn't been fighting. He'd been reaching. The sword was only the nearest tool.

Jianxin swung, and the sword was different. Each cut reached past where it landed, leaning toward something past the end of the blade, hungry with a thing a sword had no business carrying. A servant crossing the courtyard with an armload of linen slowed without meaning to, and stood holding the linen a moment too long before moving on, and could not have said what she'd seen.

Jianxin didn't notice her. He was somewhere else, the courtyard thinned to almost nothing, a carriage at the end of every stroke that wasn't there, the want of a dead man bleeding faint into his arm. He held it a while, swinging, letting it thicken, and set it down, and reached for the next.

The afraid one was harder. A younger man, soft hands, clothes that had never been good, new to this. During the fight his eyes had kept going to the treeline and his weight kept shifting back, the whole of him already leaving while the trained surface kept moving. When Jianxin swung for him the notion came split, two directions at once, the forward motion of a man still committed and the backward pull of a man already gone, fighting each other inside the single stroke so the blade wanted to do two incompatible things and did neither cleanly. He swung a dozen times and never got it to settle. The man had never settled. That was the whole of him, a person divided against his own action, and the sword that came from him carried the division because it hadn't resolved in life either.

He set it down too, unfinished. Some things were truer unfinished.

He moved through the others as the morning warmed, the follower whose intent had no center, the big quiet one whose every motion had been an apology for being there, the two who'd only made sense as a pair and that he set aside as a problem to come back to. Some gave him almost nothing, half a second glimpsed in the chaos, and the swings that came from those were thin and probably wrong, built from guess rather than reading. He kept those separate in his mind, marked them uncertain. A sword built on a guess would fail when it met the truth.

Fourteen of them were dead.

He came to that quietly, somewhere in the middle of the morning, working through a man he'd put down himself. The reconstruction went the same as the others, the clothes and the hands and the history, the notion gathering at the edge of the blade. But this one would never do anything to contradict it.

The living men he was reading might still surprise him. Any of them might turn out other than what he'd deduced, because a living intent never finished, it moved and remade itself and changed its mind, and there was no last version of a person, no bottom you could finally reach. You could read one the rest of your life and never come to the end, because the end kept moving, because they kept becoming. This man had been like that on the road, a will reaching in a hundred directions at once, inexhaustible. And Jianxin had stepped into the gap between two of those directions and closed every one of them at the same time. The snapshot was the whole of him now, fixed, the ceiling and the floor in the same place, and the reason it would stay that way forever was Jianxin.

He held that swing longer than the rest. The front-pusher's hunger and the afraid one's division had been the men as they were, read off what they'd left in the air. This was different. This was a thing he had done. It did not sit the same as the others, and he did not think it was going to, and he understood now what his arm had been doing in that half-beat before the first of them, when it balked. Some part of him had seen the whole of a person an instant before the rest of him ended it. It had tried to stop. It had been too slow, and after that there had been no time to be anything but too slow. He would carry the fourteen as this world carried all its dead, completely, and without release.

He moved on, slower than before.

---

By midmorning the household had woken around him, staff crossing on errands, a groom leading horses, two kitchen girls carrying something heavy and arguing cheerfully about whose fault its weight was. Jianxin kept working, and reached for them as he worked, idly. They were different from the thirty-two. The dead men were fixed points, each one a single intent caught and held. The living ones shifted moment to moment, the groom's attention sliding from the horses to a girl across the yard to the ache in his own back, the kitchen girls' argument carrying six small intents a second under the words. He couldn't catch and hold them. They wouldn't sit still. He could only read them as they moved, the reading dissolving and reforming with each shift, and he liked it better. The dead men were a study. The living ones were the thing itself.

One of them was watching him. A girl at an upper window, the one who'd looked at her own hands when he asked for somewhere to sleep, the daughter of the house. She wasn't pretending to be doing something else, as the staff did when they slowed to watch the stranger swing a sword. She was just watching, openly, with the whole of her attention, not yet having learned that watching is a thing you do where no one can see. He reached for her without meaning to and found nothing managed in her at all, no surface, no second thing running under the first, only a child looking at something because she wanted to see it. After a morning spent among the fixed dead and the guarded living, it landed on him like cool water. He marked it, and did not let her see that he'd noticed, and kept working, and she kept watching.

He was deep in a form, half his attention on a swing built from the front-pusher and half on a stablehand across the yard, when the world changed.

It came as a pressure under everything, the animal awareness of where the weight in a room is, pulling at him like a tide. The qi in the courtyard was moving, not violently, not visibly, but he felt it in his sternum and his back teeth and the hand on the sword, every mote of it finding the same direction, the courtyard door. The grass near the door lay flat. The groom had stopped, his horse's ears back. The kitchen girls had gone quiet without knowing why.

Jianxin lowered the sword and turned.

Tianyi stood at the door, and the world around him was not the same world that filled the rest of the yard. The air answered him. The fire in the nearby brazier bent faintly toward him without wind to explain it, and the ground under his feet was more present than the ground under anyone else's. He looked faintly tired and entirely himself.

"Fourth level," he said. "It went faster here. The qi is denser inside the walls."

Jianxin looked at his brother and felt the weight of him in his own teeth and sternum, and knew with complete certainty that if they fought right now, he would lose. Not narrowly. Completely.

Tianyi had the mountain with the steps cut into it. Jianxin had fourteen dead men he would carry, a wall in his own gift he couldn't yet climb, and a sword that was nobody's but his. He would not have traded.
