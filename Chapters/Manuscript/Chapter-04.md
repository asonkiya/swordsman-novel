# Chapter 4

By twelve Jianxin had read most of the library that had no business being in an institute library, and was working through it a second time for the things he'd missed.

The morning he found the fisherman, he was sitting in the courtyard with a folk tale open on the stones and the sword across his knees. The story was nothing, four pages, the kind of thing told to children to make them eat. A fisherman cast his net in the wrong waters every day for thirty years, and the village laughed at him, and one day he didn't, and the catch he brought in fed the coast through a famine. That was the whole of it. No moral the teller bothered to state.

He read it as he'd learned to read Garson, looking past the words at the man who'd arranged them, and what he found was not the patience the story wanted him to find. It was something harder. The fisherman had known the right waters the whole time. He'd cast wrong on purpose, every day for thirty years, because the right waters belonged to a richer man's boats and a poor man who fished them would be drowned for it quietly some night, and so he waited, casting where he was allowed to cast, until the year the famine emptied the coast of richer men and the waters came open and no one was left to drown him for taking them.

It was not a story about patience. It was a story about a man who knew exactly what he wanted and spent thirty years pretending he didn't, so that he would still be alive on the day he could reach for it.

Jianxin stood, and swung.

The first cut was wrong. He felt it as he'd felt the wrong note in calligraphy, the blade reaching for something it hadn't earned. He set the book where he could see it and went again, slower, and let the fisherman come up under the motion. The sword stopped going where the opening was. It went where the opening would be in three exchanges, and waited, casting at the wrong water, patient and apparently foolish, until the better line came open and no defense was left standing to drown it.

It took him the whole morning. By the end the cut still came apart one swing in three, the want of a man he'd never met bleeding faint into his arm and then slipping out of it again. But it arrived, twice, clean, and he marked the place in the book and set it on the shelf in his room beside Garson and the dozen others, each one a person folded down into a motion, each one his and no one else's.

This was the work of the seven years, though it never felt like work. He built sword arts out of the dead and the forgotten and the made-up. The guard of a woman who'd written letters to a man who never answered, all that contained wanting in the patience of her brushwork. The footwork of two brothers who couldn't agree how to divide a field, neither able to bring himself to win. Each one came hard, misfired more than it landed, and went onto the shelf only when it would hold. The instructors watched him at it with expressions he couldn't read, and after the third year he stopped trying to read them. Whatever they were, they were more than they looked, and he set that in the category of things to think about later.

What he could read was Tianyi, and from Tianyi there was always plenty.

---

In the same years his brother became something remarkable even by the standard of people told all their lives they'd be remarkable.

Jianxin watched it from the side, because the side was where the institute kept him. Alchemy arrived in a month. Formation theory in three weeks. The cartography instructor ran out of things to teach inside a week and spent the rest of the year being asked questions he could not always answer. The cooking instructor, brought to teach medicinal cuisine and the most unimpressed of any of them at the start, stopped correcting Tianyi after the third session and started asking him things. Each domain came, was swallowed, and within weeks Tianyi was making things the original practitioners had never reached. Not someone who had learned a thing. Someone who had understood it deeply enough to find its edges and push past them.

Jianxin watched it for years, and somewhere in the middle it stopped being funny.

He had tried to keep it funny. There was something absurd in the sheer speed of his brother, and the absurdity had been enough to stand behind for a while. But a person could only watch the world handed to someone so many times before the joke wore through to what sat under it. Every domain Tianyi swallowed was one more place Jianxin would never go. Not for want of talent. He had talent. In any other house he would have been the one they told stories about. Here he was the brother, the one who came attached. He would set his hand to a thing and be good at it, better than anyone he'd ever meet beyond these walls, and then look up and find Tianyi already standing at the far end of it, looking back, waiting with a patience that wasn't unkind and was somehow worse for not being unkind.

He'd found, around the fifth year, the one ground his brother could not cross onto. The sword had no ladder to climb two rungs at a time, no domain to be devoured whole. It had to be built, slowly, out of whoever was building it, and it was the only thing in the world that was his and not a smaller copy of his brother's. The sword was enough. He needed it to be enough. He told himself so, and most days the telling held.

The Go boards gathered dust in the common room. They had not played since the night Jianxin first beat him, years gone, both unwilling to learn what a game between them would now say.

---

Tianyi asked for one in the last month.

He asked without ceremony, setting the bowls down as though the years of dust had never happened. Jianxin took black and went first, as he always had, and reached across the board for the mind under the moves, as he always had.

It was there, the same as ever. And it no longer helped.

The reading had always been the one floor Tianyi could not calculate his way beneath, the feel for where a will was leaning before it leaned. But Tianyi had gone off to learn the game without him in it, years ago, and he had learned it. Now when Jianxin reached for the lean of his brother's intent he found it already accounted for, folded into the position before he could use it, the single advantage he'd ever held turned into one more value on a board Tianyi read better than he did.

The game was not close. Tianyi did not make it cruel. He only played, and the board filled, and the space Jianxin needed kept not being there. When it was over Jianxin looked at the stones a while.

"There it is," Tianyi said, without triumph, closer to an apology.

"There it is." Jianxin set the lid on the bowl.

He went out to the courtyard after and swung the sword until his arms gave, because it was the last thing left that his brother could not simply learn, and he did not mean to lose it as he'd lost the rest.

---

The last week, things he'd stopped noticing started coming back.

The stained glass threw its colors across the arena floor in the early morning, warriors and sages in fragments of shifting light, and he walked through it slowly, where for years he'd walked through it without looking.

And then the light itself. The thought arrived sideways one morning and stuck. He had never once seen where it came from. Sixteen years of days under a sky with no sun, and at night no stars, only the deep starless blue, and he had never thought to ask why. He asked now. Nothing answered. The question did not go away. He told himself it was the kind of thing a person simply never gets around to wondering, and knew, even as he told himself, that this was not true, that he had been kept from wondering it, the keeping as quiet and total as a sound you've stopped hearing, and that the keeping was itself the strange part. He let it go because it was too large to hold and found it would not stay let go.

There were other things. A traveling instructor, one of the few who came and went, had spoken once near the end of a year about the eastern reaches, where the land climbed toward the slope and a thing was climbing with it that the caravans had stopped routing anywhere near, a predator that grew as it climbed and that the older men spoke of with a gesture, two fingers crossed, not a word. Jianxin had filed it. It came back to him that week with the light and the sky, all of it part of one shape he could feel the edge of and not the size.

Tianyi spent the last week in the library. Not studying. Jianxin checked once and found him at a table with a closed book and his eyes on nothing, and left without a word.

The night before their sixteenth birthday, neither of them slept. They sat in the dark arena, the glass just colored panes without light behind them, the worn floor the same as it had been for seven years.

"Are you ready," Jianxin asked.

Tianyi thought about it properly. "I've prepared for this for seven years. I should be more prepared than I'll ever be." A pause. "Preparation and readiness aren't the same thing. Out there the things I don't understand outnumber the things I do, for the first time since I was small. I need to understand a thing before I can work with it, and out there I'll be working with things I don't understand yet. Constantly."

"You'll figure it out."

"Yes. That's not what concerns me. What concerns me is the time between not knowing and figuring it out."

"You'll have me for that part."

Tianyi didn't answer. Then: "That helps."

It was enough. They sat until the glass began to grey, and did not sleep, and the knock came before dawn.

---

The senior instructor stood in the corridor, the one who had been there since the beginning, who sometimes stood in the garden at dusk looking at the sloped horizon with an expression Jianxin had never been able to get beneath. He knocked once and both doors opened at the same time, which he seemed to expect.

"It's time," he said. "Take what you need. Leave what you don't."

Jianxin's room took five minutes. The sword on his back first, then Garson, then the storybooks he hadn't finished with, the fisherman among them, then clothes. He looked at what remained, the desk where the candle had burned to nothing years ago, the cot, the shelf of assigned texts he'd read and wouldn't need, and felt very little about leaving it. The things that mattered were already in the bag.

Tianyi appeared in the doorway with his own bag and a small sealed package that hadn't been there the day before, the clan's delivery, arrived overnight without anyone delivering it. He set it on the desk and looked at his brother.

"Big day," Jianxin said.

"You know you don't have to come." Tianyi said it without inflection. "You figured it out. Years ago, probably. Your path isn't bound by the restriction mine is. The outside Dao doesn't threaten you the way it threatens me. You could go straight to the clan, to our parents, anywhere, and it wouldn't matter. I've known for a while that you knew."

"Around year four." Jianxin picked up his bag. "Give or take."

"And what did you decide."

"I didn't decide anything. There wasn't a decision to make." He said it simply. "You're my brother. Where you go I go. The clan can have opinions about it."

Tianyi looked at him as if checking whether it was true. It was. He tucked the package into his bag and shouldered it.

They walked out together, down the corridor that had been theirs for seven years, past the common room where the Go boards sat dusty on their shelf, through the main hall where the morning light came through the glass and painted its fractured colors on the floor one last time. Jianxin didn't stop to look. He'd looked enough.

They walked through the gate.

Behind them the institute was gone. Not demolished, not abandoned. Simply no longer present. The lot stood empty, the stones already absorbed back into the ground, the stained glass warriors and ancient sages dissolved into the morning air. Seven years of walls and classrooms and a common room table with a wobbly left leg, all of it returned to wherever the clan's things went when they were done being needed.

Jianxin turned to look once, found bare ground, and turned back to the world, which was very large and starting now, and did not wait for him to be ready.
