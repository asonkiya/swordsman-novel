# Chapter 2

The two brothers made their way into a gray stone building no older-looking than the children themselves, eight or nine years old, built in the shape of a cross like the places of worship that dotted the world beyond the walls, and differing from them only in the abject lack of worship inside. Where a cathedral would have set its windows full of gods and angels, this one held legends of warriors and ancient sages, light falling through the stained glass to paint fractured colors on the floor. There was no altar. No incense. At the center, where a nave belonged, there was a wide open space that felt more like an arena than a place of devotion.

Jianxin had worked out early what the place was for. Not in any one moment. There had been no telling, no sitting-down. He had worked it out as a child works out the weather, from a hundred small signs that meant only one thing together. The instructors had been sent. The supplies came. The walls had gone up around something, and the something was his brother. He'd asked once, small enough to ask plainly, why they lived where they lived, and the answer had been about Tianyi. Every answer was, if you followed it far enough. He hadn't minded. He'd filed it and gone back to whatever he was doing.

"You want another game of Go before classes?" Jianxin threw it at his brother's back, a grin spreading. "I'll go easy."

Tianyi disappeared into his room without breaking stride.

It was a fair goad. For all that Go rewarded the kind of calculation Tianyi was built for, Jianxin beat him at it every time. In smaller games Tianyi still won. But as the board grew, something in Jianxin shifted that didn't run on calculation at all. He would set a stone where it looked like a mistake, and you understood it three moves later, when it turned out to answer an attack you hadn't yet thought to make.

The day's classes did nothing to carry it off. The spar sat under everything, running itself again behind whatever the instructors were saying, and he answered when he was asked and caught maybe half of it. By evening it had stopped being a question he was turning over and become one that was turning him.

That night he came back to his room with it still unsolved and dropped onto the narrow bed. Swordsmanship manuals lay open and face-down across the floor and stacked along the walls, spines cracked, half of them library books that had never found their way back. Training swords stood in the corners and lay where they'd been set down, several of them snapped and splinted with cord and lacquer and put back to use.

He lay tracing it out with a finger against the blanket, replaying the sequence in the obsessive way his mind had with things it couldn't resolve. There was no reason the last strike should have failed. From the set of his legs to the angle of his grip, his form had been the kind you could lift into a textbook. He had done everything correctly and Tianyi had turned it aside like it was nothing, and he could not find the flaw.

He gave up on sleep and sat up.

On his desk, under a set of scrolls his weapons instructor had assigned and which he had not opened, was a slim manual he'd found wedged between two other texts in the library. He'd taken it for the fact that it had no title and no author on the spine. Only inside the cover, in a hand so precise it looked typeset: *Garson.* Below it a date, and nothing else.

He picked it up and flipped through it standing, the once-over he gave anything he expected little from.

The first section was case notes. A merchant accused of falsifying weights, the evidence, the witnesses, the ruling. He skimmed. The second was the same, a land dispute laid out like a proof, possibilities eliminated until one remained. Jianxin went looking for the place where the judge had cut a corner or leaned on a guess, and there wasn't one, and that annoyed him more than a flaw would have.

*Cold,* he thought, dropping back onto the bed with it. *The man writes like he's never met a person in his life.*

He read it again anyway.

By the fourth case he had stopped looking for flaws, though he wasn't sure when. His thoughts had stopped running alongside the text and started running inside it. He was no longer reading Garson's conclusions and weighing them. He was watching Garson think, tracing the order possibilities were considered in, the weight each was given, the moment each was let go. A disputed inheritance, five heirs with partial claims. Garson opened not with the strongest argument but with the one most likely to muddy the rest, collapsed it, moved on, and by the time he reached the real claim two thirds of the tangle was already gone.

Jianxin read it again. The same order, the same sequence of release. He felt his own attention narrow, and it had nothing to do with the cases and everything to do with the shape underneath them. He didn't notice his grip on the manual loosen, or his sword hand drift off his knee to his lap, or his shoulders curl forward, settling into the slump of years spent at a desk, his body expecting nothing else of itself.

The last section was not cases. It was notes Garson had set at the margins of his own work, short and addressed to no one. Jianxin read one.

*The question is never what is right. The question is what remains when everything that cannot be is removed. What remains is the answer. It was always the answer. The deliberation is only the process of seeing it.*

He read it again, then again. His objections came slowly and left faster. He would find an edge to pull at and reach for it, and find Garson had quietly closed that door three sections back, and the next, and the one after that. There was nothing to argue with. There was only the reasoning, and the slow recognition that he had run out of places to stand outside it.

His sword arm moved, not because he decided to but because within the structure of that thought it was the next thing. The strike that had failed in the garden, he could see now exactly where the flaw had lived, the opening it left, what an opponent like Tianyi would always do with space like that. The blade came up slow. The grip was wrong, too loose, too far back, the wrist angle something his instructor would have winced at. It moved with none of the music that made Jianxin's sword his.

But it arrived. The blade stopped an inch from the wall with the unhurried finality of a door being closed, carrying no more force than the conclusion required.

Then the clarity broke. It did not collapse. It simply stopped being there. One moment his thoughts ran in a single clean channel and the next they didn't, and he was aware of the room again, the wrong angle of his wrist, the unfamiliar heaviness in his shoulders, the fact that he was sitting like a man twice his age who'd forgotten how to move. He straightened, and felt his spine remember itself.

*So that's what it is.* Not agreement. Not possession. The shape of someone else's mind, worn briefly like a coat cut for a different body entirely.

He set the manual down. He was not sure what to do with it yet. The strike had worked, that much was clear, and the way it had worked bothered him more than if it hadn't, because he didn't understand the mechanism well enough to repeat it. It had arrived through a dead man's certainty moving down his arm, and he couldn't read a judge's case notes before every fight. He sat in the dark a while, grinning at the size of the thing he hadn't learned yet, and at some point, still grinning, he slept where he sat.

---

The bell found him already awake, at the desk, with no memory of moving there, the candle burned down to a pool of wax around its own base.

Tianyi knocked twice, as he always did. "You look like you slept on your desk."

"Something like that." Jianxin fell into step, tucking his shirt. "You ever read something and feel like the person who wrote it was right there in the room?"

Tianyi looked at him sideways. "You didn't sleep."

"He was better company."

The literature class met in a room of bowed shelves. The instructor, a thin man whose relationship with enthusiasm had been strained by decades of repetition, read a stanza he had read too many times to hear it any longer, then began moving through it line by line. They were studying a poem old enough that its author had passed into historical fact, reduced to the handful of biographical notes at the front of every anthology.

Tianyi answered most of the questions, his responses precise and complete, the kind that close a question rather than open it. When the instructor asked what the central image communicated, Tianyi said without hesitation that the withered tree in the final stanza was the cyclical nature of decline, reinforced by the seasonal imagery in the second and fourth lines. The instructor nodded, his brush moving in the register as he spoke.

Jianxin leaned a seat over toward his brother. "He's not wrong," he murmured. "He's not right either." It earned him a sideways look and nothing else.

He was looking at the poem and wondering why the tree was in winter and not autumn.

Autumn was the obvious season, the one every reader reached for when writing about decline. But bare branches were a different thing from falling leaves. Something already finished, not something in the act of finishing. The poet had needed the grief to be old grief, gone quiet, the weight of having outlasted a thing rather than the act of watching it end.

He caught himself mid-thought and sat straighter. It was the same motion he'd done with Garson. Not the same depth, nothing like the narrowing in the small hours. But the motion was the same. He hadn't been reading the poem. He'd been reading whoever wrote it, and he hadn't noticed himself do it until it was done. Which meant it was not the circumstances. It was something he did. Had always done, apparently, without knowing to call it anything.

He raised his hand before the instructor finished the next question.

"The tree is in winter," he said, when the man pointed at him, "not autumn. That's the thing. Autumn would be about watching something end. Winter means it's already over. Whoever wrote this wasn't in the middle of losing something. They were on the other side of it. The grief is old. The kind you've mostly made your peace with. Not entirely. Mostly."

The room was quiet. The instructor set his brush down and ground a little more ink than the lesson would need. "That is one interpretation."

"What's yours?"

"The cyclical nature of decline." Tianyi's exact words. He moved on to the next question.

He wrote nothing in the register for it.

Tianyi, one seat over, had gone still, with the look he got when a piece would not fit.

---

The calligraphy room had better light and wide low tables, and each place had been set with paper and ink before the students arrived. The instructor placed a mounted piece at the front, a single phrase, two characters, written in a hand that made Jianxin stop what he was doing.

He understood why it was the model. The proportions were exact, the stroke order flawless. But underneath the form, in the pressure of certain strokes, heavier than the shape required, someone had pressed down harder in places. Not errors. Decisions. Someone had felt more strongly about parts of the phrase than others, and it had come through whether they'd meant it to or not.

He wanted to know who.

Tianyi began at once, and after three attempts his characters were near indistinguishable from the model. Technically. Setting them beside the original was setting a copy beside the thing it copied and feeling the absence of whatever had made the original worth copying.

Jianxin picked up his brush and tried to do on purpose what had happened twice now without trying. He reached for the mind behind the strokes as he'd reached for Garson, and found nothing but the pressure of his own effort and a blank page. He leaned in. He narrowed his eyes. He tried harder, which made it worse. He set the brush down and looked at the ceiling.

Tianyi glanced over. "Are you alright?"

"I'm trying to find someone." It did not clarify things.

He looked at the mounted piece again without reaching for anything. Just looked. The two characters together. The weight in the strokes. And then it came, faint, more a change in temperature than of state. Whoever had written this had been proud of what the phrase said. Not the kind of pride that announces itself. A certainty in the heavy strokes, the mark of someone writing words they'd earned the right to believe.

He picked the brush back up and pressed down in the same places, not because the form asked for it but because the person who'd written it had needed to. His characters came out uneven, the proportions off, his attention split between technique and the thing under it. The instructor passed, paused at Tianyi's work with a nod, crossed to his.

She looked at it without speaking. Then: "Your form needs work." And moved on.

It wasn't praise. It also wasn't the nod she'd given his brother, and he couldn't decide whether the difference meant anything or whether he was reading meaning into the pause of a woman deciding which note to write first. The uncertainty was new. He was not used to being unable to read a room.

The next class was qi-arrays, a heavyset instructor walking through the logic of a channeling diagram. Tianyi answered a question the man hadn't finished asking, naming not only the answer but two failure points that would propagate from the first disruption, and the instructor wrote them down without acknowledging they were past what he'd asked.

Jianxin tried his trick on the array and hit the blankness he'd learned to expect from old formations, too many hands across too many years, the first intent buried so deep there was nothing left to read. He gave up and switched to the ordinary approach, which was where the trouble started.

At the things the institute had been built to teach, he was bad. Not late to bloom, not slow to start. Bad. The formations slid off him like water off glass. He could recite the elemental tables and never once feel them. When the instructor put a question to him directly he gave the answer of a boy who'd been keeping up by his fingernails and had just felt them slip, and across the room his brother had already answered something two layers beneath it that no one had thought to ask. In a class of two students, Jianxin was the second one, reliably, by a distance you could measure with a stick.

He'd stopped being stung by it years ago.

Mostly.

It was the same in every room, and he had long stopped noticing it. The instructors came alive for Tianyi. Not warmly, none of them were warm, but their attention sharpened when he spoke, the sharpening of a craftsman handed material worth the work. They had been sent here for him. On the days the indifference landed somewhere, a small flat weight, Jianxin noticed it and set it down and did not let the room see.

They walked back in the early evening, the light going amber through the trees beyond the walls. At the door of their rooms Tianyi stopped.

"What were you thinking about. In literature."

"Whoever wrote the poem." Jianxin shrugged. "He already knew what the tree meant. I was answering the more interesting question."

Tianyi looked at him, and something in him caught and reset, like a piece that would not fit anywhere on the board. He said nothing more, which from Tianyi said more than most things he put into words, and went into his room to start the night's work.

Jianxin stayed in the corridor a moment with the two characters still in his mind, and the poem, and the blank wall the array had given him. Three things in one day. Two of them had opened. One had stayed shut. He found, turning it over, that he could not yet tell what made the difference, and that not knowing was the most interesting thing that had happened to him in a year.
