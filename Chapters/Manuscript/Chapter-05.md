# Chapter 5

The senior instructor was waiting at the forest's edge, and looked as though he could have waited there all day and minded none of it. In his hand was a sealed package smaller than the one the clan had sent Tianyi. He held it out to Jianxin.

"A moment," he said. "Alone."

Tianyi walked into the trees without comment.

"You understand you're not required to go with him," the instructor said, his voice the same measured thing it had always been, carrying the quality Jianxin had never been able to get beneath. "The clan has made arrangements. You could be with your family by the end of the month."

"I know."

"His path will take years. Difficult ones, most likely. The world is not arranged for anyone's convenience."

"I know that too."

The instructor looked at him with an expression Jianxin still couldn't read, opaque as the first day. Then it shifted, small enough that he almost missed it. "Then I've nothing else to say. Except that the Madam is pleased to see her sons support one another." A pause, shorter than the first. "She misses you both severely. She would like very much to speak with you. When you're able."

He said it without the measured quality the rest of his sentences carried, and it was the first time in seven years Jianxin had gotten anything from him at all. He set it away somewhere he'd find it again.

"Thank you," Jianxin said. "For all of it."

The instructor nodded once, more than Jianxin had expected, and Jianxin walked into the trees where his brother waited. Tianyi looked at the package, then at his face, and asked nothing.

---

The forest was enormous, and this was not a thought Jianxin had expected to have. He'd grown up knowing the world was large, had read about forests and plains and the territories of distant cities, had understood in the abstract that the institute was a small piece of a large world. The understanding had stayed abstract until the trees were actually around him and the institute was actually gone.

"It's big," he said.

"The institute was four hundred meters at its widest. A forest runs for kilometers in every direction." Tianyi paused. "The difference is significant."

"Thank you. Very helpful."

Tianyi had already stopped listening. He was reading the trees, the undergrowth, the angle of the light through the canopy, his head tilting as he built whatever picture he was building, and the tension he'd carried in his shoulders since before the gate loosened as he worked. The world was a thing that could be read, and he was reading it, immediately and completely himself in a place he'd never been. Jianxin watched him and felt the simple pleasure of it.

He touched the bark of the nearest tree, then the one after. Different textures. He hadn't known that.

"What are you doing," Tianyi said, not looking up.

"Touching the trees. I've never touched a tree before. Outside the grounds."

"They're the same trees."

"Different bark."

Tianyi came over, put his hand on the first, then the second, and his face shifted into the look he got when his model needed updating. "The soil's different on this side of the slope. That would change the bark." He moved on. "Interesting."

They walked. After a while the silence settled into the comfortable kind, both of them busy noticing things. Jianxin picked up a stick and hit a tree with it, a satisfying sound, and did it again.

"What are you doing now."

"Hitting a tree with a stick. Because I can."

Tianyi looked at him, then picked up his own stick, tapped a tree once with mathematical precision, and set it down. "All right. I see the appeal."

Jianxin laughed, and it came out louder than he expected with no walls to hold it, and a bird took off in alarm. He watched it go, and reached for it without deciding to, and found nothing he could use, the same absence he'd found with the deer in the grounds. He'd known he would. He still tried.

"There's no one to read out here," he said.

"We'll find people."

"I know. Just noticing."

---

By the second day they had a rhythm, and it was not, Jianxin would have admitted if pressed, an even division of labor.

Tianyi found the water. Tianyi read the slope and the drainage and set their shelter at the one angle that kept the wind off them, a wind Jianxin hadn't known was coming. Tianyi cooked what Jianxin brought back and didn't char it, which was more than Jianxin could say for the one time he'd tried. Tianyi cultivated. Jianxin could not yet cultivate at all.

What Jianxin could do, it turned out, was cut things. He could bring down a rabbit, more or less, and skin it badly enough that Tianyi did the skinning after the second night without either of them discussing it. And he could make firewood. He was, in fact, extraordinary at making firewood. He could take a fallen branch apart into clean splits faster than any woodsman with any axe, reducing good deadfall to kindling with a blade forged for finer work than this. Tianyi pointed it out once. He did not point it out again.

Beyond that the forest had no use for him. Everything it asked, his brother answered. And the one thing Jianxin was for, people and the reading of them, the forest did not hold a single example of.

He didn't mind. He was out in the world he'd wanted for seven years, and being useless in it did nothing to dim the wanting.

"The terrain has a consistent incline," Tianyi said, on the second evening, frowning at the ground. "Barely there. But present."

"Hills?"

"The gradient's wider than hills this size would give." He pressed his palm flat to the dirt, the way he had with the bark. "If it's a single rise, the scale would be unfathomable. Larger than any natural mountain. Which isn't possible." He held it a moment longer than the conclusion needed, then took his hand off the ground. He did not reach for a tidier answer this time. He didn't say anything. He let the thread drop, and Jianxin watched him let it drop, and saw that it was not a shrug. His brother had set the thing down because it had begun, faintly, to frighten him. And what frightened Tianyi enough to drop, Jianxin would not be able to leave alone.

Jianxin looked at the ground, which looked like ordinary ground. He'd lived his whole life on this world and stopped feeling the slope entirely. It was strange to have it under his feet now that he knew to feel for it, and stranger that it ran one way, always, gently, up toward a center he'd never seen, as the light came from a source he'd never found.

He didn't say any of that. He picked up the rabbit and started a fire he was, at least, excellent at building.

---

That night Tianyi sat cross-legged in the dark and cultivated for the first time, and Jianxin watched from the lean-to.

He'd asked, earlier, what it would be like. Tianyi hadn't been able to say. When he opened his eyes an hour later he tried.

"You know the difference between reading about a place and standing in it." He looked at his hands. "It's like that. But the place is inside you. The texts use gathering language, drawing inward, filling. That's wrong. The qi doesn't move toward you. You make something real, something that lived only as understanding, and you bring it across into the world, and a real thing costs something, and the qi is simply present because the cost has to be paid." He paused. "The meridians were the surprise. I thought they were channels qi happened to use. They're not. They wake when the real thing appears and organize the flow toward it, the way roads organize a country. Roads that were always there, waiting to have somewhere to send things."

"Did it hurt?"

"No. It was the most natural thing I've ever done." He said it without wonder, as a fact, and lay down, and was asleep as he did everything, efficiently.

Jianxin lay awake longer. He'd watched his brother step, in an hour, onto the bottom of a road that ran up out of sight, and felt no envy in it, only the old familiar shape of the gap, and under that, tonight, something new. The strangeness of the place pressed at him. No people anywhere, and the dead the forest made, the rabbits, the bird he'd startled into the sky, sat oddly with him when he reached for them and found the silence where a thing had been. He'd expected reaching for a dead thing to feel like reaching for the deer, an absence of intent. It felt instead like an absence with an edge, as though something that should have gone had not finished going. He turned it over and could not name it, and put it with the light and the slope, in the growing pile of things about this world that would not sit quiet.

---

On the fourth morning Tianyi stopped walking, and Jianxin almost walked into him.

"What."

Tianyi held up a hand, his head tilted a fraction, his hearing catching something at the edge of its range. "Fighting."

Jianxin listened. The forest gave him nothing. But he was already orienting toward it, some sense under hearing telling him the world ahead was different from the world behind. People. Actual people, doing actual things to each other.

"How far?"

"Far enough that it's been going a while. Several combatants. Not settled." He'd already adjusted his bearing. "This way."

Jianxin was moving before he finished the sentence. The forest thinned, the canopy broke apart overhead, and between the trees a road appeared in pieces, packed earth, wheel ruts, the marks of regular use. A carriage sat at the roadside at the wrong angle, stopped hard. Two escorts held a perimeter on their feet, and the numbers pressing them were wrong by a wide margin, dozens of men, more arriving from the treeline on the far side. The escorts were buying time and running out of it.

Jianxin's hand was already on his sword.

"We don't know anything about this situation," Tianyi said beside him.

"No." Jianxin kept walking toward it, and for the first time in four days he was grinning.
