# Chapter 9

The days in the forest had a rhythm.

Tianyi navigated and built and cultivated. Jianxin hunted and swung his sword at things and tried to read things that couldn't be read. They ate. The fire went down. The starless sky sat above the canopy the same as it always had.

By midday of the first day Jianxin had established the fundamental problem with forests: nothing in them had intent. He'd tried twice, reaching for the living things around him, and found exactly nothing. A deer crossed fifty meters ahead and he reached for it automatically and found hunger and alertness and nothing he could work with.

He went hunting instead, which at least produced dinner.

He also found that killing things with a sword was satisfying in a way that killing them with a bow wasn't, and spent more time than was strictly necessary working out the most efficient angles for different approach speeds. Not because he needed to know this. Because the sword was there and the forest was full of things to hit and he had nothing else to do with his hands.

---

They made camp at the better of the two shelter sites Tianyi had identified. By the time Jianxin returned with two rabbits, badly skinned, a lean-to stood at the edge of a small clearing, correctly angled, positioned for wind, close enough to the stream for water and far enough to stay dry. Tianyi had apparently retained all three of the field construction texts he'd read during the institute years.

"You didn't have to do all that," Jianxin said.

"No," Tianyi agreed.

Jianxin handed him a rabbit. Tianyi cooked it with a precise application of fire qi that left the meat cooked through without charring the outside. Jianxin ate it and said nothing about this.

They ate. The forest settled into its evening quiet and the sky went from orange to purple to the deep familiar blue. Jianxin poked at the fire for a while and then picked up a stick and started moving through the forms, slow and loose, not working at anything specific, just feeling the shapes.

"Have you tried reading the animals?" Tianyi said.

Jianxin kept moving. "Tried a deer. Nothing useful."

"A deer should be simpler than a person. Less complexity, fewer variables. If anything your approach should work better."

"That's not how it works."

"Why not."

He finished the form and lowered the stick. "It's not about complexity. A deer doesn't have intent. It has hunger and fear and instinct, but none of that is intent in the sense I can work with. Intent requires someone deciding something. A deer doesn't decide anything. It just is."

Tianyi considered this. "So the limiting variable isn't cognitive complexity but the presence of deliberate will."

"Something like that."

"That's a significant constraint on your path."

"Only out here." Jianxin tossed the stick into the trees and looked at the treeline. "How far do you think we are from people?"

Tianyi opened the cultivation manual the clan had sent him, read for a moment, then closed it and looked at the treeline. "There's a stream gradient that suggests drainage toward lower terrain to the northeast. The undergrowth density has been decreasing since midday, which usually indicates prior human clearing."

He turned back to the manual. "Northeast. Two days at the outside."

"Fine. Northeast." Jianxin lay back and looked at the canopy. The leaves moved slightly in a wind he couldn't feel from the ground. "Are you going to try tonight?"

"Yes."

"Tell me what it's like after."

Tianyi didn't answer, which meant he was already somewhere else.

---

Jianxin's breathing evened out. Tianyi moved outside and sat cross-legged in the dark.

The forest was quiet. He let it be quiet and turned his attention inward.

The dantian first. He'd been aware of it his whole life, present and backgrounded, noticed only when he stopped to notice it. Now he stopped. It sat below the sternum and slightly inward, a space that wasn't quite a space, and his physique had been doing things in there without his direction since before he could walk, the qi moving and accumulating and organizing itself around something deeper than his body.

That deeper thing was the Dao embryo, and it was not what other cultivators had. He'd read enough to understand the distinction. Most people cultivated through a spirit root somewhere in the body, a physical node that interfaced with the world's Dao, something you could locate and feel with your hands if you knew where to press. His was nothing like that. The Dao embryo sat in his soul, beneath the physical entirely, at a depth that had no coordinates. Where the spirit root was a door, the Dao embryo was the absence of a wall, the place where his comprehension and the Dao touched without anything between them.

He reached for it deliberately for the first time, and found it waiting.

What he was attempting was this: to take something that existed only in the yin, as understanding, as abstraction, as the accumulated weight of everything he'd ever known, and bring it across into the yang. To make it real within the dantian rather than merely known within the mind. Not to show it to the world or produce it outwardly. Just to hold it as something actual, to be the place where the known thing became a happening thing, where the map and the territory folded into each other.

For the material he reached into everything he had. Sixteen years of instruction, every domain the clan had considered worth teaching him, every text absorbed and cross-referenced and pushed past its stated edges until the edges weren't where the text had said they were. All of it sitting in the yin, patient and complete, the accumulated virtual of a life spent learning. He felt the weight of it settle around him like a room he hadn't noticed he was standing in.

From all of it he selected cold air and flame. Simple. Familiar to the point of tedium. He'd known this interaction since his second year, had turned it over so many times in his mind that every variable felt worn smooth. He chose it precisely because it bored him. If his understanding wasn't coherent enough to make even this real, he needed to know that now.

He brought it toward real.

The crossing was not a single moment. It was a gradient, the virtual phenomenon becoming incrementally more actual within the dantian, the abstraction gaining specificity, weight, presence. He felt the Dao embryo working as a bridge, the yin-side knowledge passing through it and arriving on the yang side as something that existed rather than something that was known. The two states occupied the same location simultaneously for a fraction of a second and he was aware of both at once, the map and the territory superimposed.

Then the meridians lit.

He hadn't expected that. The texts described meridians as channels but he'd imagined something passive, existing infrastructure that qi used incidentally. What he felt was more deliberate than that, the network activating as a system the moment the real phenomenon appeared in the dantian, roads lighting up across his body with sudden purposefulness, organizing themselves to direct the incoming qi efficiently toward the point of demand. Not random flow finding its own way through him. Infrastructure doing its job, signposts and roads, the qi moving along them through a designed system rather than across open ground.

The qi arrived. His dantian, which had been accumulating passively his whole life, now had something real in it that required sustaining, and the world beyond his skin was responding to that requirement without being asked. The sensation was weight without mass. The phenomenon he had known as an abstraction had become an actuality, and actuality cost something, and the cost was being paid from the outside in, structured and directed and clean.

For eleven minutes he held it.

Then the real began its return to the virtual, as it always would, as it always must. He didn't hold it. He released it, not loss but completion, the yang fulfilling itself by giving way to yin. What remained was what had been required. The qi that had flowed to sustain the real had nowhere to return to now that the real had passed, and so it stayed, settled into the dantian like the silence after a bell has rung, present in the absence of what had called it.

He sat with it for a moment.

Then he opened his eyes.

Jianxin was watching from the lean-to, sitting up, arms on his knees.

"Well?" Jianxin said.

Tianyi considered. "You know the difference between reading about a place and standing in it."

"Yes."

"It's like that. But the place is inside you."

Jianxin was quiet for a moment. "That's almost a good description."

"It's the best I have." Tianyi looked at his hands. The residue in the dantian was small but it was there. "The texts use gathering language. Drawing inward, accumulating, filling. That's not what it is. The qi doesn't move toward you. You make something real and the qi is simply present because real things require it." He paused. "The meridians are the interesting part. I hadn't understood what they actually did until now."

"What do they do?"

"They organize the flow. Without them the qi would still come but it would have no direction. They're the infrastructure. The roads." He stood. "I'll try something more intricate tonight."

---

The second day was the same as the first except Jianxin got better at skinning rabbits and Tianyi worked through six distinct things in the dantian, each more intricate than the last, and by evening the effort was sitting in him differently than it had the night before, less strain in it, more weight.

The third day Jianxin challenged Tianyi to a spar because he was bored and because combat in an open forest was different from combat in the institute's arena and he wanted to find out how.

It was different. Tianyi's elemental control had more material to work with out here, the wind moving through the trees in currents that could be redirected, the earth underfoot with variation and root systems and soft patches that could be made relevant, the fire with fuel everywhere. He was more dangerous than he'd been in the controlled space of the institute, and Jianxin had to work faster to stay ahead of the environmental pressure.

But faster was fine. Jianxin liked faster.

He found the edges of what Tianyi was doing within the first twenty exchanges and spent the next forty minutes mapping the specific ways the forest's variables made the web stronger and where they introduced new gaps. By the time they stopped he had three new things he wanted to try and a developing theory about why Tianyi's approach had a specific weakness against opponents who planted fixed points rather than responded to positions.

He didn't share any of this. It was more useful unshared.

Tianyi, sitting against a tree with his hands on his knees, said: "You found something."

"Maybe."

"You have that look."

"I don't have a look."

Tianyi didn't argue. He was right and they both knew it.

They ate. Tianyi cultivated. Jianxin lay on his back and looked up at the canopy and moved his sword hand slowly through forms without thinking about them, the blade still sheathed, just the motion, the familiar shapes passing through his arm.

He thought about people. Not the instructors, not Tianyi. Strangers. People who didn't know he was coming, who had no reason to have prepared a surface for him, who would just be themselves in whatever way they were themselves. He found himself moving through forms without thinking about it, the blade still sheathed, just the shapes, his body already rehearsing something the rest of him hadn't named yet.

Soon.

On the morning of the fourth day Tianyi stopped walking.

Jianxin almost walked into him. "What."

Tianyi held up a hand. His head had tilted a fraction of a degree, the angle of someone whose hearing had found something at the edge of its range.

"Fighting," he said.

Jianxin listened. The forest gave him nothing. But he was already orienting toward the sound, some sense underneath hearing registering that the world ahead was different from the world behind.

People. Actual people with actual intents, doing actual things to each other.

"How far?"

"Far enough that it has been going on for a while. Multiple combatants, not settled." He had already adjusted his bearing. "This direction."

Jianxin was already moving before Tianyi finished the sentence.

They moved. The forest thinned, the canopy broke apart overhead, and between the trees ahead a road appeared in pieces, packed earth, wheel ruts, the signs of regular use. A carriage sat at the roadside at the wrong angle, stopped hard. Two escorts on their feet, holding a perimeter, but the numbers pressing against them were wrong by a wide margin. Dozens of men, more still arriving from the treeline on the far side. The escorts were buying time and running out of it.

Jianxin's hand was already on his sword.

"We don't know anything about this situation," Tianyi said, beside him.

"No," Jianxin agreed, and kept walking toward it.
