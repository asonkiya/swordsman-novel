# Chapter 15

They were back in the guest quarters before the household's evening had finished. Jianxin sat with his sword across his knees and didn't do anything with it. Tianyi read. Neither of them said anything about the council, or Casver, or the road.

There was nothing left to say about it. The road was still blocked. That was the whole of it.

The days after passed quietly. Tianyi spent them in the library. Jianxin spent them in the courtyard with the sword.

On the sixth day Maren arrived at their door with four tailors, two attendants, and a look that did not invite discussion.

---

The tailors worked fast. Jianxin stood where they put him and let them move around him. Their eyes went to his shoulders, his arms, the line of his back, never his face. He was built the way the sword wanted him built. Not more of everything, more of the right things in the right places, the shoulder and the forearm and the long muscle running down the sword arm all exactly what years of one specific kind of work made them. The rest of him followed, lean and in service to it. One of them ran the tape across his shoulders, looked at what he had, and took the measurement again, not because it was wrong, but because it was particular, and particular things had to be gotten exactly right.

He looked fine. He moved fine. It was only if you watched him the way the tailors watched, the professional eye running over weight and line and how a body held itself at rest, that something registered as slightly unresolved, the whole of him oriented around a point just past his hip where nothing was. Not wrong, exactly. Complete in itself, but completed around an absence.

Across the room they were having an easier time of it. Tianyi gave them nothing to correct. Where his brother was built toward one thing, he was built toward all of them and none in particular, every part of him brought up together, even, in proportion, the body of someone whose training had been complete rather than aimed at a single use. The tape said what they expected it to say, the first time, every time.

They were building something layered onto him, deep-colored, each piece sitting where it was meant to sit, the outer robe cut to make a tall man taller. Every component did something. The whole of it was elevated, distinguished, correct in the way everything Tianyi did was correct. The tailors looked pleased with themselves.

The thing they'd laid out for Jianxin had a great many pieces. He picked it up, turned it over, put it down. He had nothing to compare it to and no opinion about whether it was correct. It only seemed like a lot of things trying to be noticed at once.

He took a simpler piece off the rack instead. One dark layer, a single line of deep amber at the collar, nothing else.

"That isn't what we prepared," the head tailor said.

"I know. This one."

The tailor looked at the piece, then at him, and started the adjustments without arguing.

When they finished and the attendants had gone, the brothers stood in the cleared room.

It was the same face on both of them. From across a room they were one man twice, same height, same build, same jaw, same set of the brow. Up close it came apart. Tianyi wore the face still and settled, the composure of a man who had never once had to put it on, because it was only what he was; behind the black eyes the room was already a board with the pieces counted. Jianxin wore the same face like it might do anything next, lighter, looser, something of the child still at play in it even now.

Jianxin looked at his brother, his own face arranged into a stillness he never saw in it, and grinned.

"You look like you're about to rob someone," Tianyi said.

"I look excellent."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

Jianxin laughed. Outside the window the light was going gold over the city and the estate was waking into its evening, and the two of them waited for it to begin.

---

They were announced at the top of the stairs.

Jianxin felt the room change as they came down. A small lull, conversations losing their thread, eyes finding them and staying. Nobody here knew anything about them, which meant nobody knew how to place them, which meant for a moment the room simply looked.

The young women looked at Jianxin. Not the careful glances the evening was built for but the other kind, heads turning before their owners had decided to turn them. The older guests looked at Tianyi. They took their time about it, reading how he stood, how he held the room without seeming to want it, and a woman by the far wall leaned to the woman beside her and said something, and both of them kept their eyes on him while she said it.

Tianyi had already lost interest in being looked at. He was reading the room itself, the seating, the empty chairs at the head where the family would sit, the shape the evening would take once they did.

They reached the floor. Neither of them had ever done this. The room ran on rules they'd never been taught, and Jianxin could feel himself learning them by the second, fast, the same as he learned anything that came at him through people.

Tianyi found a place near the wall where he could see all of it. Jianxin walked toward the nearest knot of guests.

---

It was easier than it had any right to be. He hadn't expected that. He was only interested in them, each one a different thing to figure out, and the interest did the rest before he'd decided to show any.

There was a young man from some provincial house who'd been holding himself stiff all night, every sentence checked before he let it go. Jianxin asked him where he was actually from, not the version for the room, and watched the stiffness go out of him mid-answer. By the fourth exchange he was laughing. He'd spent the whole evening trying to look like he belonged and forgotten to once someone treated him like he already did.

There was a woman Jianxin's own age with her whole situation written in the things she stepped around. A marriage, someone she hadn't met, an evening spent being fine with it. He didn't touch any of that. He asked about one small thing she'd let slip without meaning to, and for a few minutes she was just talking, present, herself. When he moved on she watched him go and could not have said what had happened, only that she wanted more of it.

There was an older merchant with no real standing here who'd tucked himself against a wall to make it look like a choice. Jianxin went and stood with him and started talking, and within a few minutes three other people had drifted into the conversation and the merchant was somehow at the center of it. None of them could have told you how. They just followed where it went.

A woman cornered Tianyi to find out whether he'd suit her daughter. She asked after his family, his prospects, his intentions. He answered every question, exactly, and reached for the next one. No warmth, no chill, nothing performed. Four minutes later she knew everything she'd asked and felt no nearer to him than when she'd started. She told her friends he was very polite. What she meant was that it had been like talking to a wall that answered correctly.

Lira was by the musicians, watching. She'd been watching the two of them since they came down the stairs. When Jianxin caught her at it she looked away fast, then back, the looking-away a beat too late to have worked.

She was terrible at it. He almost said something, remembered she was twelve, and let her keep watching.

---

The family came in together.

Lord Aldric first, the room turning toward him on its own. Lady Isara beside him, her smile arriving on time, her attention spread out evenly across the room with nothing left to accident. Aldren behind them, easy in a way he never was in the council chamber. Cael half a step out of the evening's rhythm, in the room without quite belonging to it. He had the look of a man who had made his position clear and been overruled and was now required to stand in a room celebrating the thing he'd argued against. Maren last, and by the time she entered the evening was already running on something she'd set in motion hours ago.

Jianxin watched the room sort itself around each of them. It bent one way for Lord Aldric and another for his children. It bent for Maren too, in the small constant ways that meant she was the one running it, though no one in the room seemed to know that was what they were responding to.

He read her in the doorway. The composure was complete. Underneath it, faint, was a woman watching a thing she'd built to see whether it would hold. It was holding, and she wasn't letting herself feel that yet.

Cael was looking at her too. Not the same read. The same subject, a different answer. Whatever he'd said and lost, he hadn't finished losing it.

He looked away before she felt him looking.

---

The dinner was the part that stayed with him.

Not the silver or the candles, though those were beautiful. It was the people. He kept reading them and finding the same thing in every one, and it took him a while to name it.

Nobody here was working anything out. The vassal who couldn't stand the man on his left didn't have to decide what to do about him; the table had been set, and the setting decided it, and that was all that was asked. The guest who'd rather have been anywhere else didn't have to sit with that feeling; the evening told him what to do and he did it and the feeling never came up. No one was feeling their way toward anyone. No one was guessing at what they owed or what a glance had meant or what any of it cost. All of that had been settled before the first guest arrived.

It pulled at him in a way he hadn't expected.

Every person in that room knew what they were and where they stood and why it mattered, and not one of them had to wonder about it. The vassal across the table kept his obligations without once asking whether they were worth keeping. Lord Aldric never questioned that the room was his; the room agreed it was, and so he moved through it like a man who'd never had a sleepless night in his life. Even the bad blood in the room had edges you could see. Cold, but clear. Everyone knew where they stood with everyone.

No one was searching for anything. Jianxin had spent his whole life in a place where nothing came settled, where every answer had to be dug out from the beginning, and standing in a room full of people who had simply never had to do that, he understood, for the first time, why a person might guard it with their life.

Tianyi appeared at his shoulder.

"You've been standing here four minutes."

"I know." Jianxin looked at him. "Are you enjoying yourself?"

"I don't think that's the purpose."

"It's a party. That's the entire purpose."

Tianyi looked at the room like a man who'd received different information and wasn't going to be talked out of it.

"Go and talk to someone," Jianxin said.

"I have been talking to people."

"For longer than five minutes."

"Length isn't the point."

Jianxin laughed. Tianyi found something in the room more interesting than the conversation and drifted toward it, and Jianxin watched him go, straight toward where Maren was standing, and thought that would be the one worth watching tonight.

---

Maren had put herself near the east wall, where she could see all of it. Tianyi arrived at the same spot a few minutes later, having wanted it for the same reason.

They stood a while without talking. It wasn't uncomfortable, which neither of them remarked on.

"Drevant will hold tonight," Tianyi said eventually.

"You think."

"He can't move on you with Venne in the room. Not in the open. Not with a witness who'd make it cost him." He paused. "You sat them together on purpose."

She didn't say yes. She didn't have to.

"After tonight is the question," he said.

"After tonight is always the question." She watched Drevant across the room, the care he was taking not to land his eyes anywhere too long. "He's been backing away for a month. I can't find why."

"The northern route."

She looked at him.

"His land sits on it. It's been dying for years. He can't say so without saying the house is weaker than it was, so he says other things. The road dispute. The points of order. Same complaint, different clothes."

"I knew about the route." She said it slowly. "I never set it beside the rest."

"It isn't only him. Half the houses here are taxed on roads that emptied out a decade ago. The ones who quietly moved their trade are fine. The ones who didn't are going under and calling it luck."

"That isn't in the records."

"It's in the convoy logs. The guild filings. You have to read them next to the tax rolls, and nobody does, because the men who keep one don't talk to the men who keep the other."

"You did."

"I had the time."

She held his eyes a moment longer than she needed to, then turned back to the room.

"There's a thing under that, too," he said. "The numbers coming up from the districts. They don't hold together. Not by much. But they all lean the same way."

"The administrators."

"They're not lying. They're surviving. A district whose numbers look bad loses something, so the numbers never look bad. Everyone shades a little, all in the same direction, and no one calls it dishonest because everyone's doing it and the books still balance at the end."

She was watching him now with an attention she hadn't given anything else all night.

"And the people who decide things," she said, "decide off numbers that were already bent before they got there."

"Bent by people who believe in the thing they're bending. That's what makes it hold. A man who doubted would check his figures twice. They don't doubt, so they don't."

She let out a breath that was almost a laugh and had nothing funny in it. "You can't tell them. Tell them the numbers are wrong and you've told them the whole thing they've trusted their entire lives is wrong."

"And no one here survives saying that."

"No." She looked out over the room a long moment. The candles, the silver, the vassals in their seats. "Two years I've been in those records. Finding small things. Sure I was missing the big one." Quieter. "There was no big one. Just the small ones. All leaning."

"You weren't missing anything."

She glanced at him, and this time whatever crossed her face she didn't quite catch in time.

"Vorn posted surpluses four years running," she said. "Their roads were gone by the second. I raised it twice. I was told their records were in order." A pause. "They were in order. That was the trouble. They were always going to be in order."

"Because the other thing meant admitting the years before were lies."

"Yes."

They stood with it.

"So what do you do," she said. Not for the sake of asking. She wanted his answer.

"You don't fight the numbers. You build a better set beside them, quietly, and you let it sit there being right until people start reaching for it on their own."

"That takes years."

"Yes."

The corner of her mouth moved. "You say that very easily."

"It's a solvable problem. Those are the easy ones."

"And the ones that aren't?"

"Those are interesting for other reasons."

She looked back at the room, and didn't speak for a moment, and when she did it was lower, nearly not to him at all.

"I've never said any of this out loud. Two years." A breath. "My father trusts the numbers. My brothers don't read them. The vassals keep their own." A pause. "Until just now I wasn't sure it was real."

He let the silence sit where it was.

"It's real," he said, when it was time.

"I know." She straightened, and the composure came back over her smooth and complete. "One thing at a time."

Across the room, Drevant was still being careful with his eyes. She watched him a moment, and this time she wasn't bracing against him. She was reading a man who was going under and didn't want it seen, and a thing understood sat differently in her hands than a thing she'd been afraid of.

"The Edrath delegation," Tianyi said. "Someone on their side will say something tonight. They usually do, at these occasions. A comment about standards, about what the house is presenting."

She looked at him. "You expect it."

"I've been watching the man by the east window for twenty minutes. He has opinions about the evening." He paused. "When it happens, Casver will use it. Whatever ground shifts tonight, he'll move on it by morning."

She was quiet. "The dormancy provision."

"Needs to be settled before he does. Not to stop the engagement. To ensure it doesn't hand him anything."

"Then it needs to move faster than I planned."

"Yes."

"The provision your father stopped using," Tianyi said. "I want the older records. I think it lapsed earlier than you think. By the better part of a year, maybe."

She went still. "If that's true—"

"I'll tell you when I'm sure."

She looked at him. "Why are you doing this. Any of it."

He thought about it before he answered, completely, the only way he knew how.

"Because it can be fixed," he said. "All of it. The numbers, the routes, the thing your uncle is doing, the marriage you're being pushed toward to hold a house that's cracking. None of it is fate. It's a structure that stopped fitting and nobody rebuilt. I can see where every piece has slipped." He looked at her. "Give me the room to work and I can make this hold. Properly. So it carries weight again instead of barely standing. So you can stop holding it up alone."

It was the most he'd said at once all evening. She looked at him for a long moment.

"You'd do that," she said. "Rebuild a house that isn't yours."

"Houses are just structures. I'm good with structures." A pause. "And you shouldn't have to be the only thing keeping this one up."

"Most people who can see the cracks use them."

"Most people," he agreed, and left it there, and she didn't press.

A small silence settled between them. Not uncomfortable exactly, but present. She looked out at the room and he looked at the room and neither of them said anything for a moment.

"He's been working the room all night," she said. "Why aren't you."

"If he can't get something out of them, I won't."

She looked at Jianxin a moment longer, then back at Tianyi. "You believe that completely."

"Yes."

She held his eyes a moment, then looked back at Jianxin across the room. Just in time to watch a certain twelve-year-old make her careful way toward him through the thinning crowd, ears red, jaw set.

---

He looked down at her when she arrived, at the red ears and the set jaw and the eyes that wouldn't quite meet his, and understood the whole thing at once. A child with a crush, braced for him to laugh at her.

He didn't laugh. But it was a near thing, and it was fond.

"Do you want to dance?" she said, fast, before she could lose it.

"Yes," he said, and held out his hand.

The first steps were wrong and he knew it the instant he took them. His weight wanted to fall somewhere it wasn't supposed to, the old balance still set for a sword, not a partner. He moved through it anyway and felt exactly where it failed.

Then he looked at the floor.

The other dancers turned past him and he read them. The shift of weight before the turn, the half-beat pause where one partner waited on the other, the small pressure of a hand that told a body where to go next, the good pairs speaking to each other without a word and the stiff ones saying nothing at all. It came at him fast and bright, every movement flaring up in front of him and going into him at once, the rise and fall, the turn, the give and take of it, all of it learned and folded in between one step and the next.

By the second pass he wasn't fumbling. By the third he was leading, and the dance had opened up under him the same as a person did when he finally saw them whole, and the people nearest them had started to glance over.

Lira felt it happen against her hands and looked up at him, eyes wide.

"You couldn't do that a minute ago."

"I can now."

"How?"

"I watched."

She stared at him like he'd done a card trick she couldn't catch the seam of. Then she laughed, surprised out of her nervousness, and forgot for a moment to be shy about it.

They turned through the next figure. He could feel her working up to something, the questions stacking behind the small ones.

"Where did you grow up?" she said. The small one first.

"A long way from here. In the mountains. There was a school, of a sort. Just me and my brother and the people who taught us."

"Only the two of you?"

"Only the two of us."

She thought about that. "That sounds lonely."

"Sometimes. We had the whole mountain, though. And nobody told us what we were supposed to want."

Her eyes came up at that, quick. "Nobody told you what you were supposed to want."

"No."

She considered that, slow and serious, and he watched her arrive somewhere, and when she spoke again it was quieter.

"Everyone tells me what I'm supposed to want," she said. "All the time. They don't say it like that. They say I'll understand when I'm older." A pause. "Maren says it now too. She didn't used to."

"No?"

"She used to argue with Father. About things she thought were unfair." Lira frowned at some point past his shoulder. "Now she just finds the cleverest way to do what he wants and everyone says how much she's grown." The frown deepened. "It doesn't look like growing to me."

"What does it look like."

"Like she went quiet somewhere inside and never came back out." She said it simply, the way she said everything, and it was the most exact reading of Maren he'd heard from anyone in the house, including the ones who'd known her all her life.

She was quiet a moment. Then, not dramatic, just asking: "Is it going to happen to me?"

He looked at her a long moment. The clear eyes, the open face, a person this house hadn't finished with yet.

He turned her through the next figure while he decided how honest to be. He decided all the way.

"It'll happen to you if you let it," he said. "It's happening to her because she's decided there's no other door. That every road's already been walked for her and all that's left is to walk it well." He held her eyes. "You don't have to decide that. Not now, not ever."

"What if there isn't another door."

"Then make one."

She blinked at him.

"If you look and there's no door," he said, "you tear one open yourself. In the wall. Wherever you want it." He turned her through the figure. "That's all a door is. A hole someone decided to make."

She looked around the room without meaning to. The dinner, the ceremony, the house that had stood for generations and would stand after everyone in it was gone. "These walls look pretty solid."

"They always do." He smiled. "Right until someone puts a blade through one."

"I don't have a blade."

"You will." He said it lightly, but he meant it, and he let her see he meant it. "And when you decide to use it, you swing first. That part's yours. Nobody can do it for you." A beat. "But once you've swung, I'll help. That's a promise."

She held his eyes a long moment, twelve years old, something settling into her that she had no words for.

Then the music turned bright and a knot of young guests swept between them, and a girl her own age caught Lira by the hand and pulled her off, and Jianxin was left standing alone at the edge of it.

He hoped she'd remember it when she was old enough to use it.

---

By the third pass the people nearest them had started to glance over. Not at Lira. At him. The boy who hadn't known the steps twenty minutes ago and was now leading. The boy who had no name anyone in this room could place, no house, no standing, no reason to be on this floor at all. The glances were not hostile. They were the glances of a room running its accounting, locating what didn't fit and noting it.

Lira didn't notice. She was talking, asking something about the mountains, and he answered and kept moving and felt the glances accumulate without doing anything about them.

The comment came from near the door. Not whispered. Said at the volume of something meant to carry.

"One does wonder what Lady Maren is thinking," the man said, "letting the help get familiar with the young lady of the house at a formal occasion." He had come with the Edrath delegation, minor family or senior attendant, someone whose function was to represent the standards of the house he traveled with. "If the Vayne household runs this loosely, Lord Edrath's family may want to reconsider whether the match suits their standing."

The room heard it. These rooms always heard it.

Lira heard it. Her hands tightened on his once, fast, and then she stepped behind him. Not a decision. The body finding cover before the mind had caught up. She had become the thing being talked about, and the person in front of her was the only solid thing in reach.

He stood still and let her.

Maren crossed the room. Not fast. Fast would have confirmed something. At the pace of a woman with somewhere to be, which happened to be here. She reached them and put herself between Lira and the room with the same motion she used for everything, smooth, inevitable, already done before anyone had decided to watch.

She said something to the Edrath man that Jianxin didn't catch. The composure was total. Whatever it cost her, the room would not see it.

Lira stood behind her sister's shoulder and felt the hands of the house close over her like water closing over a stone, quiet and total and without asking, and something planted itself in her chest that she had no name for yet. Not anger, not grief, not quite fear. Something that needed more years and more weight before it would become what it was going to become.

"Though one does wonder what Lady Maren is thinking, letting the help get familiar with the young lady of the house at a formal occasion. If the Vayne household runs this loosely, Lord Edrath's family may want to reconsider whether the match suits their standing."
